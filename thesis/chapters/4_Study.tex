\chapter{Computational Study}\label{chapter:study}
This chapter tests \gls{drispi} on the 100 XL instances of Queiroga et al.\ (2026)
against the eight monolithic solvers benchmarked there. The study aims to answer two questions:
The first is whether an adaptive decomposition framework that learns its
decomposition and routing choices during a run can match state-of-the-art
monolithic solvers at this scale. The second is whether the weights HAOS learns along the way provide interpretable 
insights about the instances they were learned on.

Section~\ref{sec:stu-experiment} describes the dataset, specifies implementation and
hardware specificiations, the parameter values, and explains expertimental design choices. Section~\ref{sec:stu-results} shows and analyzes the results. Here we answer the primary and secondary research questions through comparison with the published solvers, break the results down by instance characteristics, examine the learned HAOS weights and the convergence of individual runs, and attribute the improvement to the stages that produced it.

\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:stu-experiment}
All 100 instances are solved by the same \gls{drispi} configuration. Nothing is tuned
per instance by design without anyexceptions. Section~\ref{sec:stu-dataset} describes the instances and the reference solutions used as baseline, section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation} the software and hardware the campaign
ran on, section~\ref{sec:stu-hyperparameters} the parameter values, and
section~\ref{sec:stu-repetitions} the number of seeds and the statistical precision it buys for our experiment.

\subsection{Dataset}\label{sec:stu-dataset}
The XL set (section~\ref{sec:lit-problem}) extends the generation scheme of the X
instances of \citet{uchoaNewBenchmarkInstances2017} to up to 10,000 customers. Every instance
places its nodes on a $[0, 1000] \times [0, 1000]$ grid with Euclidean distances
and combines one level of each of four attributes. The depot is Random, Central
at $(500, 500)$, or Eccentric at $(0, 0)$ in the bottom left corner. Customers are Random, Clustered, or
Random-Clustered around a small number of seed points. Demand follows one of
seven distributions: unitary (U), four ranges of increasing width (1--10, 5--10,
1--100, 50--100), a quadrant-dependent range (Q), or many small and few large
demands (SL). The target average route length $r$ lies in one of seven bands,
from ultra-short ($r \in [3, 5]$) to ultra-long ($r \in [50, 200]$), and
capacity $Q$ is set so that a solution with the minimum number of routes attains
it. The instance name references $n$ and that minimum route count, e.g.\
XL-n4535-k1134. This $k$ equals $\lceil \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i / Q \rceil$, making it the mathematical minimum number of routes, because fewer vehicles could not carry the total customer demand under capacity set to $Q$. This $k$ is
fixed by the benchmark and unrelated to the subcluster count $k$ of Section~\ref{sec:fra-haos}.
Table~\ref{tab:xl-summary} breaks down the above mentioned characteristics on a per-instance basis along with \gls{drispi}'s result and comparison to initial and current \gls{bks} values.

The initial BKS values of \citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} come from the solver
runs described in section~\ref{sec:lit-large-cvrp} and were released as baselines with the
CVRPLib BKS Challenge, a community competition opened on January~12, 2026, in
which any team can submit an improved solution for automatic verification. By
the current record,\footnote{\url{https://galgos.inf.puc-rio.br/cvrplib/index.php/en/bks_challenge/score/instances}, accessed September 7, 2026.} the challenge has improved 99 of the 100
initial values, leaving only XL-n1094-k157 to still hold its initial value. We report the
percentage gap to baselines such as the initial and the current \gls{bks}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Gap} = 100 \times \frac{c - c_{\mathrm{BKS}}}{c_{\mathrm{BKS}}}.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The quantity $c$ is the reported solution cost. The current
\gls{bks} represents the official best known solution. However, we primarily compare against initial \gls{bks}, because published solvers produced it in two-hour single-thread runs on specified hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:lit-large-cvrp}), using us an experimental design we can reproduce to allow for any algorithmic comparability. The official BKS values on the other hand have been achieved without constraints regarding computational, with many of them coming from day-long runs that rely on raw compute, rendering any fair comparison infeasible.

\subsection{Framework Implementation}\label{sec:stu-implementation}
All experiments in this chapter run on a single, shared department server under
Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS with two Intel Xeon Platinum~8280L CPUs at 2.70\,GHz
(28 cores each, 56 cores and 112 threads total) and 5,928\,GiB of RAM, with no
GPU\@. \gls{drispi} runs inside Docker containers (Docker 28.1.1) pinned to a disjoint physical-core range with \texttt{--cpuset-cpus}. This pinned CPU count, not a library thread-pool default, sets the framework's parallelism budget.

The campaign presented in section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} is comprised of all 100 instances, each run on three seeds with a wallclock runtime budget and termination criterion of T=2h (300 runs, 7{,}200 seconds each). For that we pin 48 of the server's 56 physical cores as six exclusive 8-core Docker containers, three per socket, parallelizing as outlined in section~\ref{sec:fra-pipeline} with a split into six DRI workers, one BG-AILS worker, and one SP worker. SMT siblings are left idle. Runs are packed into waves ordered by instance size so that the three seeds of the largest instance, XL-n10001-k1570, never share a wave. The full campaign takes 50 waves and about 100 hours of wall-clock time.

\gls{drispi} uses Python 3.11, with dependencies locked via \texttt{uv}: Libraries PyVRP 0.13.3 (HGS-CVRP), scikit-learn 1.8.0 (K-Means and Agglomerative Clustering), scikit-learn-extra 0.3.0 (K-Medoids), scikit-fuzzy 0.5.0 (Fuzzy C-Means), gurobipy 13.0.1 (SC/SP), and vrplib 2.1.0 for instance parsing. AILS-II, FILO, and FILO2 are vendored as git submodules pinned to a fixed commit each and compiled at image build time, rather than resolved from a floating branch, so a rebuild reproduces the exact binaries behind the results in Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison}. The whole framework runs inside a Docker image built from this locked environment, so the software stack behind every reported run is fixed independently of the host it executes on.

\subsection{Replication Design}\label{sec:stu-repetitions}
The campaign runs \gls{drispi} on every one of the 100 XL instances three times, at
seeds 11, 22, and 33. The seed drives the weighted HAOS draws, the angular
offset, and the random choices inside decomposition. It is also
passed to PyVRP, FILO, and FILO2, but AILS-II has no seed flag. This makes the routing phase, BG-AILS and the post-SC/SP improvement pass independent of the seed. This makes the runs non-reproducible. Additionally, the plugged-in solvers run under wall-clock limits, so how far their search gets depends on machine load, and the worker pool
schedules subcluster jobs in no fixed order. This affects again, the entire routing phase,
BG-AILS, and the post-SC/SP improvement pass alike, so two runs at the same seed
can differ in solution quality. Disclosing the seeds therefore does not suffice
for exact reproducibility. The three runs per instance are exchangeable replications.

Because each run is stopped after 7,200 seconds (120 minutes) of wall-clock time rather than at convergence, each run resembles a terminating simulation in the sense of \citet{lawSimulationModelingAnalysis2000}: a fixed initial condition, the first DRI iteration's decomposition and routing pass (Section~\ref{sec:fra-decomposition}), and a fixed stopping rule rather than a steady state. The replication method of \citet[ch.~9]{lawSimulationModelingAnalysis2000} therefore applies.

The precision target refers to the fixed set of 100 instances, which are a design
factor \citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} rather than a sample from a larger
population, so the seed is the only randomness the design controls. Using
$\bar G$ the grand mean gap and $\bar\sigma^2$ the average per-instance seed
variance, we get $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G) = \bar\sigma^2 / (I \cdot S)$ with
$I = 100$. The $I \cdot S$ observations are independent by construction. Every
run is a separate process with its own random-number stream, and no pool, cache,
or counter is shared across runs (Chapter~\ref{chapter:framework}). This interval does
not extend to the published baselines of Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison}, which
report one value per instance and no variance.

A pilot campaign of three diverse instances with four seeds each measured a
root-mean-square per-instance seed standard deviation of 0.118\,pp at the
7{,}200-second mark. The target half-width was fixed in advance at 0.03\,pp, small
enough to resolve the 0.02--0.06\,pp effects seen in earlier component tests.
Table~\ref{tab:seed-sizing} forecasts the standard error and the 95\%
half-width of $\bar G$ at the pilot value. Every $S$ from one to four meets the
target. This would in fact still hold true at a 99\% interval, but precision alone does not decide $S$.

Three other considerations set $S = 3$. At $S = 1$ there are no within-instance
degrees of freedom and no interval at all. At $S = 2$ a discrepancy between two
runs is visible, but cannot be attributed to either of them. At $S = 3$, however, one
anomalous run stands out against the other two. We also require $S > 1$ to present a best-of-$S$ column, which is the
convention on this instance set \citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026}.

A fourth seed would add 100 runs and 1,600 core-hours to shrink the half-width
from 0.013 to 0.012\,pp. That gain is too small next to a second source of error.
Two pilot campaigns that differed only in execution environment produced means
0.039\,pp apart, three times the $S = 3$ half-width. Such a batch effect shifts
every run of a campaign alike, so more seeds do not reduce it, and the budget of
a fourth seed would not buy a meaningfully more precise $\bar{G}$
\citep{barrDesigningReportingComputational1995,johnsonTheoreticiansGuideExperimental2002}.
Neyman allocation, which assigns seeds in proportion to each instance's standard
deviation \citep{neymanTwoDifferentAspects1934,cochranSamplingTechniques1977}, would have further complicated the experimental design while only cutting the standard error of $\bar{G}$ by 8.8\% at the same total budget. Therefore, every instance receives
three seeds.

The pilot value was conservative. In the campaign, the mean per-instance seed
standard deviation is 0.037\,pp, about a third of the pilot value
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison}). The root-mean-square per-instance seed
standard deviation, which enters $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G)$, is 0.052\,pp, which
puts the realized 95\% half-width of $\bar G$ near 0.006\,pp. This
interval applies to the mean over seeds only. Best-of-$S$ is an extreme-value
statistic whose expectation depends on $S$, so it is not comparable across
studies with different replication counts. Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} uses
it descriptively and focuses its main comparison and the answer to our primary research questions on the mean-of-$S$ gap.

\subsubsection*{Why the mean and not the best of three}

Mean-of-$N$ is the primary comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}, because \gls{drispi}'s mean-of-3 and each
published solver's mean-of-60 estimate the same population quantity, the
expected cost of a single run, and differ only in how many draws the average
rests on. Best-of-$N$ does not. It is an extreme-value statistic whose
expectation falls as $N$ grows, so \gls{drispi}'s minimum over three draws stands
against the minimum over sixty for every published solver, a sampling budget
twenty times larger, and the comparison rewards the larger budget before it
rewards the better algorithm.

The size of that mechanical advantage can be read off \gls{drispi}'s own runs.
Sampling one of the three seed costs at a time gives an expected best-of-1 gap
equal to the mean-of-3 gap, $0.296\%$; the average of the smaller cost in each
of the three retained seed pairs gives a best-of-2 gap of $0.273\%$; the actual
three-seed minimum gives the best-of-3 gap of $0.264\%$ already reported in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}. The first extra sample is worth $0.023$
percentage points (pp) and the second $0.009$ pp, and the sequence is still
falling at $N = 3$. AILS-II, FILO2, FILO, SISRs, KGLSXXL, and HGS-CVRP gain
$0.07$, $0.09$, $0.10$, $0.16$, $0.21$, and $0.25$ pp respectively between
their own mean-of-60 and best-of-60 columns, larger reductions drawn from many
more repetitions. \gls{drispi}'s mean per-instance seed standard deviation is
$0.037$ pp, so additional seeds narrow its best column by less than they narrow
a higher-variance baseline's. This is what produces the rank inversion in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}: \gls{drispi} ranks third by mean-of-$N$ and fourth
by best-of-$N$ without any change in the underlying solution quality, because
the two columns reward different things. Best-of-$N$ is reported to match the
published solvers' own headline metric, not as the primary comparison.

For the mean of three runs to carry that weight, the three runs have to be
exchangeable replications rather than one good run and two filler runs. They
are. Seeds 11, 22, and 33 produce mean gaps of $0.291\%$, $0.298\%$, and
$0.300\%$, an ordering a Friedman test over the 100 matched triples does not
separate from chance ($\chi^2 = 3.61$, $\mathrm{df} = 2$, $p = 0.164$); no
pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank test between two seeds reaches significance
either, the smallest $p$ being $0.158$ for seeds 11 against 22. The ordering
also runs against the throughput one would expect to explain it: seed 33
completed the most \gls{dri} iterations of the three (8,208 against 7,730 for seed 22
and 6,865 for seed 11) and still finished last. The per-instance seed standard deviation has
a mean of $0.037$ pp and a median of $0.026$ pp, its root mean square, the
quantity that enters $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{G})$, is $0.052$ pp, and the realized
95\% half-width of the grand mean is $0.006$ pp
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-repetitions}). That spread is not uniform across the
set. It runs from $0.0005$ pp on XL-n4535-k1134 to $0.222$ pp on XL-n6034-k61,
and it tracks route length rather than instance size, a pattern
Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics} takes up with the gap itself.

\subsubsection*{Which solvers \gls{drispi} beats}

The means in Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} do not establish whether an
observed difference is real rather than an artifact of the 100 sampled
instances. We tested \gls{drispi}'s mean-of-3 column against the corresponding
mean-of-60 column of five of the eight published solvers, AILS-II, FILO2, FILO,
SISRs, and HGS-CVRP, with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test
\citep{demsVarStatisticalComparisonsClassifiers}, treating the 100 instances as matched pairs. KGLSXXL,
LKH-3, and OR-Tools are omitted: their gaps in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} are large enough that the paired comparison
would not be informative. Best-of-$N$ compares three draws against sixty and
is not the equal-sample-size comparison the paired test requires, so it is not
tested.

Each baseline is tested twice, in opposite directions, because the two claims
the framework has to support are not the same claim. The first family asks
whether \gls{drispi} is better, with $H_0$ stating that the distribution of paired
differences is not shifted below zero. The second asks whether \gls{drispi} is
worse, with $H_0$ stating that it is not shifted above zero. Rejecting the
first is evidence of an advantage; failing to reject the second is the weaker
statement that no disadvantage is detectable, which is what parity with a
state-of-the-art monolithic solver amounts to on a fixed instance set. Each
family is corrected separately for its five tests with the Holm step-down
procedure \citep{demsVarStatisticalComparisonsClassifiers}, and Table~\ref{tab:paired-tests} reports both
adjusted $p$-values side by side. The paired differences are the per-instance
percentage gaps to the current \gls{bks}, and each test uses the normal
approximation to the signed-rank statistic without a continuity correction.

Table~\ref{tab:paired-tests} splits the five tested solvers into three
groups. \gls{drispi} is significantly better than SISRs and HGS-CVRP. The margin
is $0.286$ pp against SISRs and $1.152$ pp against HGS-CVRP, and \gls{drispi} is
ahead on 98 and 99 of the 100 instances. Against FILO the mean paired difference
is $-0.037$ pp and the Hodges--Lehmann shift $-0.026$ pp, with an uncorrected
95\% confidence interval of $[-0.060,\ -0.001]$, and the uncorrected signed-rank
test rejects at $p = 0.043$; the Holm correction over five baselines leaves it
at $0.064$. On 100 instances with a paired-difference standard deviation of
$0.203$ pp, the corrected test resolves a shift of about $0.066$ pp at 80\%
power, so a difference of the size observed here sits below what this instance
set can separate. \gls{drispi} is ahead of FILO on the point estimate and on 55
of the 100 instances, and the set is too small to call it. Against
FILO2 neither test rejects: the mean paired difference is $+0.003$ pp,
\gls{drispi} is ahead on 47 of 100, and there is no detectable difference in
either direction. Against AILS-II the second family rejects decisively.
\gls{drispi}'s mean-of-3 is $0.137$ pp behind AILS-II's mean-of-60 and ahead on
only 4 of 100 instances. AILS-II found 93 of the 100 initial \gls{bks} values
\citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} and remains the strongest solver on this set by a clear
margin, so being second to it, level with FILO2, and not separated from FILO
by the corrected test places \gls{drispi} inside the leading group without
instance-specific tuning, which is the claim Chapter~\ref{chapter:framework}
sets out to support.

\subsection{Performance by Instance Characteristics}
\label{sec:stu-characteristics}

The XL instance set varies two candidate notions of scale at once: the customer
count $n$ and the target average route length $r$ (Section~\ref{sec:stu-dataset}).
The two are not interchangeable stand-ins for the same underlying quantity.
$r$ is not derived from $n$; it is an independent design factor of the
benchmark's own generation scheme, which fixes vehicle capacity $Q$ and fleet
size jointly so that a minimum-route solution attains the target $r$ for
whatever $n$ the instance happens to have, and the 100-instance design
deliberately crosses a wide range of $r$ against a wide range of $n$ rather
than letting one determine the other. Their sample correlation on this set is
$0.054$. For \gls{drispi} specifically the two also act on the pipeline
differently: $n$ sets how many customers decomposition has to distribute across
$k$ subclusters, while $r$ sets how many stops a single vehicle round trip
covers on average and, by construction, compresses the number of routes
relative to customers once it grows large.

A linear regression of the mean-of-3 gap on $n$ over all 100 instances gives a
slope of $3.12 \times 10^{-5}$ percentage points per customer ($t = 3.48$,
$p = 0.00076$, 95\% confidence interval
$[1.34 \times 10^{-5},\ 4.90 \times 10^{-5}]$). A regression that treats $n$
as the only scale variable can pick up the long-route instances through it, so
the same fit is repeated on the 86 instances with $r < 50$, the dataset's own
ultra-long route threshold (Section~\ref{sec:stu-dataset}) rather than a cut chosen
after inspecting the gaps. The slope falls to $1.65 \times 10^{-5}$
percentage points per customer ($t = 2.62$, $p = 0.011$, 95\% confidence
interval $[3.96 \times 10^{-6},\ 2.90 \times 10^{-5}]$). It stays positive and
significant, so the association with $n$ is not carried only by the 14
long-route instances, but it halves once they are removed.

Customer count and route length can be compared directly once each is measured
in sample standard deviations from its own mean. One standard deviation is
2,456 customers for $n$ and $40.4$ stops for $r$. On that common scale the
slope on $n$ is $0.077$ pp per standard deviation ($R^2 = 0.110$) and the slope
on $r$ is $0.133$ pp per standard deviation ($R^2 = 0.332$): route length
accounts for about three times as much of the variation in the gap, and its
slope per standard deviation is $1.7$ times the slope on $n$. A joint
regression on both standardized predictors leaves a partial slope of $0.070$ pp
per standard deviation for $n$ and $0.130$ pp for $r$, and the route-length
partial slope exceeds the customer-count partial slope by $0.060$ pp per
standard deviation ($t = 2.31$, $p = 0.023$, 95\% confidence interval
$[0.008,\ 0.111]$).

\subsubsection*{A third axis: demand per customer}

Capacity $Q$ and route length are set jointly by the generation scheme, so
their ratio recovers a quantity neither variable carries on its own. With
$k \approx \sum_i q_i / Q$ the minimum fleet and $r \approx n/k$, the average
demand per customer satisfies $\bar{q} = \sum_i q_i / n \approx Q/r$. On this
set $\bar{q}$ ranges from $0.9$ on the unitary-demand instances to $75.7$ on
the 50--100 instances, and its logarithm correlates with $r$ at only $-0.188$,
which makes it a third axis rather than a restatement of the first two.
On its own, standardized $\log \bar{q}$ has a slope of $0.062$ pp per standard
deviation ($R^2 = 0.073$).

Adding standardized $\log \bar{q}$ to the joint regression raises $R^2$ from
$0.423$ to $0.570$ and leaves three significant slopes: $0.147$ pp per standard
deviation for $r$ ($p = 5.1 \times 10^{-15}$), $0.090$ pp for $\log \bar{q}$
($p = 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$), and $0.069$ pp for $n$ ($p = 2.1 \times 10^{-5}$).
Route length remains the largest of the three, and customer count the smallest,
in every specification we fitted.

\caption{Mean-of-3 Gap to the Current \gls{bks} Against Customer Count, Average Route Length, and Log Average Demand per Customer. Each predictor is measured in sample standard deviations from its own mean. All three panels share the $y$-axis. Lines are ordinary least-squares fits on all 100 instances.}
\label{fig:gap-scale}

\subsubsection*{Route length also drives the run-to-run spread}

The same asymmetry appears in a quantity the gap regressions do not measure.
Regressing the per-instance seed standard deviation on each standardized
predictor in turn gives $0.022$ pp per standard deviation for $r$
($p = 7.2 \times 10^{-11}$, $R^2 = 0.353$), $0.002$ pp for $n$ ($p = 0.60$,
$R^2 = 0.003$), and a slope indistinguishable from zero for $\log \bar{q}$
($p = 1.00$). In the joint model only $r$ survives, at $0.022$ pp per standard
deviation ($p = 4.6 \times 10^{-11}$). Instance size therefore moves the level of the
gap without moving its stability, whereas route length moves both:
Figure~\ref{fig:seed-spread} shows the two panels side by side. Mean seed
standard deviation is $0.093$ pp on the 14 instances with $r \geq 50$ against
$0.027$ pp on the other 86, a factor of $3.4$, and the same band carries a mean
gap of $0.574\%$ against $0.251\%$ (Mann-Whitney $p = 0.00066$). The
long-route instances behave as a separate regime rather than as the upper end
of a single trend, which is the reason the scale claim above is stated
conditional on $r$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/seed_spread.pdf}
\caption{Per-Instance Seed Standard Deviation Against Customer Count and Average Route Length. Each predictor is in sample standard deviations from its mean, and both panels share the axes. Lines are ordinary least-squares fits on all 100 instances.}
\label{fig:seed-spread}
\end{figure}

The concentration of the largest gaps on long routes is consistent with what
decomposition does to a long route. Every clustering method in
Table~\ref{tab:decomp-methods} draws its partition from customer
coordinates or route centroids and the dissimilarity metric of
Equation~\ref{eq:decomp-dissimilarity}, without reference to which vehicle will
eventually need which customers; a long route spans a customer set spread
further apart on average than a short route serving a comparable number of
stops, and that same spatial spread makes it more likely that some clustering
cut line passes through the route rather than around it. Once a long route
straddles a cluster boundary this way, the routing stage
(Section~\ref{sec:fra-routing}) still solves each side of the cut to a good local
optimum in isolation, but the seam between them is exactly the kind of defect
BG-AILS exists to repair (Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails}). BG-AILS repairs every
seam in an instance under the same fixed chain length of $k$ cross-reconnects
and the same wall-clock budget of Equation~\ref{eq:bg-ails-budget} regardless of
how many of those seams cut through a long route rather than a short one, so an
instance whose long routes cross more boundaries has more repair work competing
for a budget that does not grow to match it. The variance result fits the same
account: a repair budget that is sometimes sufficient and sometimes not
produces run-to-run spread, which is what the long-route band shows.

\subsubsection*{The characteristics that do not matter}

Figure~\ref{fig:characteristics-panels} sets the remaining instance characteristics
against the gap: depot position, customer distribution, and demand
distribution. Of the three, only demand distribution separates the groups.
Kruskal-Wallis tests give $p = 0.153$ for depot position (three levels),
$p = 0.180$ for customer distribution (three levels), and $p = 0.00085$ for
demand distribution (seven levels); under Holm correction across the three
tests the first two stay well above any conventional threshold (adjusted
$p = 0.305$ for both) and the third remains significant (adjusted
$p = 0.0025$). Depot position and customer distribution are also
non-significant after controlling for $n$ and $r$ (partial $F$-tests,
$p = 0.76$ and $p = 0.55$). Whether the depot sits at the centre, in the
corner, or at a random point, and whether customers are scattered, clustered,
or both, does not measurably change what \gls{drispi} achieves.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/characteristics_panels.pdf}
\caption{Mean-of-3 Gap to the Current \gls{bks} by Instance Characteristic, Across All 100 XL Instances. Boxes Span the Interquartile Range, Whiskers Extend to 1.5 IQR, and Points Beyond Are Drawn Individually. In panel (b), Rand.\ clust.\ is random-clustered. Demand classes in panel (c) are ordered by median gap.}
\label{fig:characteristics-panels}

The demand-distribution effect is real but it is not a seventh independent
factor. The group means run from $0.127\%$ on the unitary instances to
$0.451\%$ on the quadrant-dependent ones, and the five classes in between
($0.250\%$ to $0.337\%$) do not separate from each other at all
(Kruskal-Wallis $p = 0.33$ on those 72 instances alone). What distinguishes
the two extremes is $\bar{q}$: unitary demand fixes $\bar{q} = 1$, so capacity
is consumed one customer at a time and a purely spatial partition is
automatically a balanced-load partition, while the quadrant-dependent
distribution makes demand a function of position, so a partition drawn from
coordinates alone cuts along a demand gradient and hands subclusters very
unequal loads. Once standardized $\log \bar{q}$ enters the model alongside $n$
and $r$, the demand-distribution grouping no longer adds anything
($F = 1.51$, $p = 0.18$), and neither does depot position ($p = 0.22$) or
customer distribution ($p = 0.98$). Three continuous quantities account for
what the categorical breakdown shows.

Each of the seven demand classes holds only 14 or 15 instances, so the
individual group means in Figure~\ref{fig:characteristics-panels} carry the per-instance
imprecision discussed in Section~\ref{sec:con-limitations} and are reported
descriptively. The tests above are run on the full set.

\subsubsection*{Instance hardness against the cost of decomposing}

A gap to the \gls{bks} mixes two things: how hard the instance is for any solver, and
what decomposing it costs on top of that. The per-instance mean-of-60 gaps of
AILS-II separate them. Fitting the same three standardized predictors to
AILS-II's own gap gives $0.091$ pp per standard deviation for $r$
($p = 6.8 \times 10^{-18}$), $0.046$ pp for $\log \bar{q}$, and $0.024$ pp for
$n$ ($p = 0.0064$). Long routes are therefore hard for the monolithic solver
too, and nearly as strongly as they are for \gls{drispi}, while customer count moves
AILS-II's gap by about a third of what it moves \gls{drispi}'s.

Fitting the same model to the paired difference isolates what decomposition
costs. All three slopes are positive and significant, and they are close to
one another: $0.055$ pp per standard deviation for $r$
($p = 4.1 \times 10^{-8}$), $0.046$ pp for $n$ ($p = 2.3 \times 10^{-6}$), and
$0.044$ pp for $\log \bar{q}$ ($p = 5.9 \times 10^{-6}$). Route length
dominates the raw gap because it is an instance-hardness axis that happens to
hit \gls{drispi} slightly harder than it hits AILS-II. Customer count dominates
neither, but what little it does to the raw gap is almost entirely a
decomposition cost rather than general hardness. Over the full observed range
of the set, $3.6$ standard deviations of $n$ and $4.6$ of $r$, the
corresponding penalties relative to AILS-II are about $0.17$ pp and $0.25$ pp.
The weak scale dependence claimed for the gap therefore holds in absolute
terms and holds less comfortably in relative ones, and
Section~\ref{sec:con-limitations} states the consequence for the primary research
question.

\subsection{HAOS Weights}
\label{sec:stu-haos}
\label{sec:results-haos}

HAOS chooses the number of subclusters $k$, the compactness weight $\lambda_q$,
the VB/RB paradigm, the clustering method, and the subsolver, one draw per DRI
iteration, and updates its weights from the rewards of
Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} (Section~\ref{sec:fra-haos}).  The clustering method is
two wheels, one per paradigm, so the five decisions are six wheels.  Whether
those weights carry interpretable information about an instance is the secondary
research question, and answering it takes two steps rather than one.  A weight
vector that has left the uniform distribution has learned something only if what
it learned is the ranking of the arms.  A wheel can also leave uniform by
reinforcing whichever arm its own early draws happened to favour, which produces
the same shape and means nothing.  The two are distinguishable, but not from the
weight vector alone: they are separated by asking whether the arms that ended
heavy are the arms that actually paid.  This section establishes the movement
first and then tests its direction.

The analysis reads the per-iteration weight snapshots the runs log for all six
wheels.  Recomputing Equation~\ref{eq:haos-probability} from the logged raw
weights reproduces the logged probabilities to a maximum absolute deviation of
$1.11 \times 10^{-16}$ across 630,004 arm snapshots, so the weight trajectories
below are read rather than reconstructed.  The ten warm-up iterations are
excluded throughout.  Runs complete between 22 and 118 iterations with a median
of 78, and 149 of the 300 reach that median, so the later part of every
trajectory describes the longer half of the campaign.

\subsubsection*{The wheels do move}

Figure~\ref{fig:haos-entropy} shows normalized entropy staying high on five of
the six wheels, and read alone it suggests the weights never leave uniform.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/haos_entropy.pdf}
\caption{Normalized Shannon Entropy of the Six HAOS Wheels Against \gls{dri} Iteration. The curve is the median across all 300 runs and the band is the interquartile range. The horizontal axis stops at iteration 78, the length of the median run. The dashed line is the end of the 10-iteration uniform warm-up.}
\label{fig:haos-entropy}
\end{figure}

The weights themselves say otherwise.  At iteration 78 the median ratio of the
largest weight on a wheel to the starting weight $w_0$ is 4.89 for $k$, 4.65 for
$\lambda_q$, 9.37 for the paradigm wheel, 2.85 for the vertex-method wheel, 3.36
for the route-method wheel, and 6.87 for the subsolver.  Arms sit exactly on the
decay floor in 2.5\% of post-warm-up snapshots on the paradigm wheel and 31.2\%
on the vertex-method wheel, and that fraction does not rise with arm count: it
is 0.21 on the three-arm route wheel against 0.10 on the four-arm subsolver.
Nearly every arm leaves the floor at some point in a run, and once credited it
stays above the floor for a mean of 31 to 40 snapshots against a re-selection
gap of 1.9 to 7.8 iterations, so an arm is credited again long before it
relaxes.  Those persistence figures are lower bounds, because between 68\% and
99\% of the spells are still open when the run ends.

The probabilities follow the weights.  The median top-arm probability at the
last iteration is 0.257 on the $k$ wheel against a uniform $1/7$ of 0.143, 0.323
on $\lambda_q$ against 0.167, 0.694 on the paradigm wheel against 0.500, 0.295
on the vertex-method wheel against 0.200, 0.481 on the route-method wheel
against 0.333, and 0.432 on the subsolver against 0.250.  Every wheel ends
between 1.4 and 1.9 times uniform on its leading arm.  The probability floors of
Equation~\ref{eq:haos-probability} are not what holds the distributions flat:
a floor artifact would show large weight ratios alongside probabilities still at
uniform, and the probabilities have moved.

\subsubsection*{The direction is arbitrary}

What the wheels have not done is rank the arms.  Within a run, the Spearman
correlation between an arm's mean realized reward and that arm's final
probability is 0.081 on the $k$ wheel, 0.045 on $\lambda_q$, 0.043 on the
vertex-method wheel, 0.030 on the route-method wheel, and 0.042 on the
subsolver.  The arms that finish heavy are not the arms that paid.
Figure~\ref{fig:haos-scatter} plots the two against each other.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/haos_scatter.pdf}
\caption{Mean Immediate Reward of Each Arm Against Its Final Wheel Probability. Each point is one arm in one run with at least five draws. The dashed line is $1/A$, drawn at each run's own number of arms on the $k$ wheel. The annotation is the mean within-run Spearman correlation; the paradigm wheel has too few arms for it.}
\label{fig:haos-scatter}
\end{figure}

Part of that is a property of the option set rather than of HAOS\@.  A
Kruskal-Wallis test per run on the per-selection rewards grouped by arm, Holm
corrected within the run, rejects on at most 6.0\% of runs on any wheel and on
0.0\% for $\lambda_q$ and the route-method wheel.  The mean within-run spread
between the best and the worst arm is 0.45 to 1.05 reward points against a
median selection reward of 5.  On the instances and budget of this campaign the
arms are close enough that no wheel has a strong ranking available to learn.
That explains a flat weight vector.  It does not explain a weight vector that
has moved to 1.4 to 1.9 times uniform on an arm chosen at random, and the second
observation is the one that matters for the research question.

\subsubsection*{Why the reward carries no ranking}

Figure~\ref{fig:haos-reward} gives the immediate reward $R$ banked by one
selection, summed over the decompose-route candidate and the BG-AILS candidate.
Of 19,803 post-warm-up selections, 16,591 bank exactly $R = 5$: the
decompose-route candidate scores the no-improvement value of 1 and BG-AILS
scores the improvement-over-previous value of 4.  A BG-AILS reward is present on
96.8\% of selections.  The median $R$ is 5 and the mean 4.94, and 84\% of all
selections receive the identical reward.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/haos_reward.pdf}
\caption{Immediate and Deferred HAOS Rewards, Excluding the Ten Warm-Up Iterations. (a) Immediate reward over 19,803 selections; 16,591 of them, 84\%, sit at $R = 5$. (b) Deferred reward over the SC/SP applications that carried one. The two panels have different denominators.}
\label{fig:haos-reward}
\end{figure}

The three-level scale of Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} therefore has no
resolution where the outcomes are.  BG-AILS almost always improves on the
previous candidate, which is what the stage is for, and almost never produces a
new incumbent best, so nearly every selection lands in the middle bucket.  The
quantity that separates a good configuration from a mediocre one on this
benchmark is the size of the improvement, and the scale discards it.  The
lift-off condition of the weight recurrence is met comfortably as a result: with
$R = 5$ against a threshold of $0.5263 \cdot A$, between 90\% and 98\% of
selections clear the bar on every wheel except the ten-arm $k$ domain, where the
threshold is 5.26 and only 5.4\% clear it.  Weights rise on nearly every arm
nearly every time it is drawn.

This is what turns a missing ranking into a confident-looking weight vector.
The smallest reward in Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} is 1.0 rather than zero, so
an arm accumulates weight for having been drawn, independently of what followed.
Weight sets selection probability through Equation~\ref{eq:haos-probability},
selection probability sets the draw rate, and the draw rate sets the rate of
accumulation.  An arm that is drawn a few extra times early compounds that lead.
The result is reinforcement of the sampling noise rather than of the outcomes,
and it accounts for every observation in this section at once: the entropy
curves dip, the leading arm ends above uniform, the three seeds of one instance
disagree at a total-variation distance of 0.2 to 0.3
(Figure~\ref{fig:haos-seeds}), and that disagreement does not fall as runs get
longer, because a reinforced random walk diverges rather than converging.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/haos_seed_agreement.pdf}
\caption{Mean Pairwise Total-Variation Distance Between the Three Seeds' Final Wheel Probabilities, Against the Instance's Mean Number of Completed \gls{dri} Iterations. The distance is $\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_a |w_a - w_b|$, averaged over the three seed pairs. The line is a LOWESS smooth.}
\label{fig:haos-seeds}
\end{figure}

A second channel points the same way: the reward has a long right tail, with 879
selections at $R = 8$ and 292 at $R = 11$, so an arm that happens to catch two
such events early carries the bump for the rest of the run.  Separating the two
channels would need a control run and was not attempted.

Three wheels are still gaining weight range between iterations 40 and 78, with
bootstrap intervals above zero: $k$ at 0.036 per iteration
($[0.027,\ 0.045]$), the route-method wheel at 0.015 ($[0.005,\ 0.025]$), and
the subsolver at 0.065 ($[0.049,\ 0.078]$).  The intervals for $\lambda_q$, the
paradigm wheel, and the vertex-method wheel contain zero.  Under the reading
above, a still-rising range is not a system approaching the right answer with
more evidence; it is an arbitrary leader becoming more entrenched.  A longer
budget would sharpen these weights without making them more informative.

\subsubsection*{Where the weight comes from}

The weight level itself is not explained by the immediate channel alone.  The
interior fixed point of the recurrence in Equations~\ref{eq:haos-update}
and~\ref{eq:haos-decay}, for a stationary selection probability $p$ and a
per-selection reward $R$ under credit-then-decay, is
$w^{*} = \gamma p R / (1-\gamma) = 19\,pR$.  Evaluated at the run-average
probability and the mean immediate reward, that prediction sits below the
observed median maximum weight by a factor of 1.70 to 3.01 on every wheel.  Two
quantities are missing from it, and both are measured.

The first is that $p$ is not stationary.  Under the reinforcement above, the
leading arm's probability rises over the run, so a run-average $p$ understates
the probability at which the largest weight was actually accumulated.  The
second is the deferred channel, which contributes 0.443 of all credit mass on
every wheel and does not appear in the immediate reward at all.  Correcting for
both, total credit per selection is $4.94 / (1 - 0.443) = 8.87$, and evaluating
$19\,pR$ at the median final top-arm probability gives 43.3 for $k$ against an
observed 48.9, 54.4 for $\lambda_q$ against 46.5, 72.8 for the subsolver against
68.7, and 117.0 for the paradigm wheel against 93.7.  The recurrence describes
the weight level once it is given the probability the weight was accumulated at
and the full credit stream.  The two conditional method wheels need a further
correction for how often their paradigm was drawn and are not reconciled here.

That deferred channel is a mechanism in its own right, and it works against
ranking.  The deferred reward is credited once to every distinct configuration
tag among the routes SC/SP selected, at the full value regardless of how many
routes that tag contributed (Section~\ref{sec:fra-sp}).  On an
iteration that carries a positive deferred reward, 1.8 to 5.3 arms of the same
wheel gain weight at once, against 0.55 to 1.34 on an ordinary iteration, and
about 62 weight units are injected against about 5.  Nearly half the credit in
the system arrives as a broadcast timed to the SC/SP schedule rather than as a
judgement about the arm that received it.

\subsubsection*{What this means for the secondary research question}

The secondary research question asked whether the learned weight distributions
give interpretable insight into instance structure and reduce manual tuning
across instance classes.  On this campaign they do not, and the diagnostics
above locate three causes rather than one.  The reward scale places 84\% of
selections in a single bucket and discards the improvement magnitude that would
separate the arms.  The smallest reward is positive, so weight accumulates with
the draw count and the roulette draw feeds that back, which converts sampling
noise into a stable-looking leader.  The deferred channel supplies 44\% of the
credit as a broadcast to many arms at once.  Table~\ref{tab:haos-causes}
collects the measurement behind each.

None of the three is a property of the hierarchy.  The wheel mechanics behave
exactly as specified, the weights move freely, and the probabilities track them.
What the campaign shows is that the credit these wheels are fed does not carry a
ranking, and Section~\ref{sec:con-future} takes up what would have to
change.  The consequence for the primary research question is stated in
Section~\ref{sec:con-limitations}: a controller whose weights are uncorrelated with
arm performance is, in effect, close to the uniform draw its predecessor used,
so the result of Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} was obtained without
working adaptation rather than because of it.

\subsection{Convergence Behavior}
\label{sec:stu-convergence}

An improvement is any candidate that strictly lowers the incumbent cost,
including the first solution of a run. The run logs record 2,335 such updates
across the 300 runs. Figure~\ref{fig:improvement-quarters}(a) counts them by the
wall-clock quarter in which they were logged, measured from the first timestamp
of each run log. A run may still be finishing the iteration already in
progress when the 7,200-second limit is reached, and the seven updates logged
during that overrun are included in the fourth quarter. Of the 2,335 updates,
1,586 fall in the first 30 minutes, 361 in the second, 232 in the third, and
156 in the fourth. Every run's first solution is one of these updates, and all
300 of them fall in the first quarter, so 1,286 first-quarter updates lower an
incumbent that already exists. Every update in the later quarters does the
same.

Counting updates overstates how much of the run is spent converging, because
the updates late in a run are individually larger than the ones early in it.
Figure~\ref{fig:improvement-quarters}(b) therefore tracks the quantity the rest of this
chapter reports. The mean-of-3 gap to the current \gls{bks} stands at $0.391\%$ at
the 30-minute mark, $0.335\%$ at 60 minutes, $0.311\%$ at 90 minutes, and
$0.297\%$ at 120 minutes, against the $0.296\%$ of
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} once the overrun updates are counted. The
gains per quarter are $0.056$, $0.025$, and $0.014$ pp. They are falling, and
they are not falling to zero: the last of the three is still about a quarter of
the first, and more than twice the realized 95\% half-width of the grand mean.

The run-level counts say the same thing more directly. Of the 300 runs, 122
improved their incumbent after the 90-minute mark and 221 after the 60-minute
mark. On 7 of the 100 instances all three seeds were still improving in the
final quarter, and on only 22 was no seed still improving. Seven runs logged
an update after the 7,200-second limit, in the iteration that was already in
progress. At the point where the budget of Section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation}
cuts the search off, \gls{drispi} is not at a converged solution on most instances;
it is at wherever the clock found it. A longer budget would have produced
further gains, and the relevant question is how much. A geometric decay fitted
to the observed quarter-on-quarter ratios of $0.45$ and $0.54$ implies a
remaining tail of about $0.013$ pp beyond 120 minutes, which would put the set
mean near $0.283\%$. That extrapolation rests on two ratios and is offered as
an order of magnitude, not as a result; the campaign to test it was not run.

Where that residual sits is the more useful finding, and it is not where the
iteration counts suggest it should be. Completed \gls{dri} iterations fall sharply
with instance size, from a mean of 117 on the ten smallest instances to 31 on
the ten largest (Spearman $\rho = -0.97$), and the natural reading is that the
largest instances are the ones furthest from convergence at the cutoff. The
improvement data does not support it. The mean-of-3 gain over the final
quarter correlates with route length at $\rho = +0.52$
($p = 3.9 \times 10^{-8}$) and with customer count at $\rho = +0.06$
($p = 0.57$). It averages $0.041$ pp on the 14 long-route instances against
$0.010$ pp on the other 86, while the split by size is flat: $0.015$ pp above
3,400 customers against $0.013$ pp below. Figure~\ref{fig:improvement-quarters}(b)
shows the two route bands separately, and the long-route curve is still
descending visibly at the cutoff where the short-route curve has flattened.

The two findings are consistent once the binding constraint is stated
correctly. Fewer iterations on large instances leave HAOS with fewer draws per
arm, but Section~\ref{sec:results-haos} finds the arms statistically
indistinguishable on all six wheels, so a configuration drawn under a shorter
iteration budget is not on average a worse configuration. The iteration count
constrains what the wheels can learn; it does not by itself leave cost on the
table. Long routes leave
cost on the table for the different reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics}: they generate more boundary repair
work than the fixed BG-AILS budget covers within one iteration, so the repair
continues across iterations and needs wall-clock time rather than evidence.
The instances with the largest gaps are therefore also the instances still
improving fastest when the run is stopped, which means part of the long-route
penalty reported above is a budget artifact rather than a defect of the
decomposition itself. How much of it is which was not tested here.

\subsection{Improvement Sources}
\label{sec:stu-sources}

Sections~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} to~\ref{sec:stu-convergence} treat
\gls{drispi} as one procedure. It is five stages, and the route pool tags every
route with the stage that produced it (Section~\ref{sec:fra-improve}), so the
improvement each stage contributes can be attributed rather than assumed. The
question matters for two reasons. The framework's contribution over its
predecessor DRSCI \citep{altendeiteringDRSCICVRP2026} rests on BG-AILS and HAOS, and the
BG-AILS ablation of Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails} measured that stage in isolation
on a fixed checkpoint rather than inside a full 7,200-second run. A stage that
carries little of the total improvement under the campaign budget is also the
first candidate for reallocating core time.

The incumbent log records 2,335 updates. Cost reduction is measured from each
run's first solution to its final incumbent, so the 300 opening solutions count
as updates and are excluded from the reduction. Stage time is the sum of the
per-stage timers. Those timers run concurrently on the core split of
Section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation}, so the time shares below partition the
recorded stage time and do not partition the 7,200 seconds.

One attribution is not recoverable as two separate stages. The SC/SP job
(Section~\ref{sec:fra-sp}) solves the pool and then runs the standard AILS pass
of Section~\ref{sec:fra-improve} on the result, and the incumbent update is
logged only after that pass, under the phase name \texttt{standard\_ails}.
All 715 updates logged under that name match an adopted SC/SP application, one
for one, so the cost reduction in that row is the reduction of the package.
The two timers are logged separately, and Table~\ref{tab:improvement-sources}
reports them that way.
