\chapter{Computational Study}\label{chapter:study}
This chapter tests \gls{drispi} on the 100 XL instances of Queiroga et al.\ (2026)
against the eight monolithic solvers benchmarked there. The study aims to answer two questions:
The first is whether an adaptive decomposition framework that learns its
decomposition and routing choices during a run can match state-of-the-art
monolithic solvers at this scale. The second is whether the weights \gls{haos} learns along the way provide interpretable 
insights about the instances they were learned on.

Section~\ref{sec:stu-experiment} describes the dataset, specifies implementation and
hardware specifications, the parameter values, and explains experimental design choices. Section~\ref{sec:stu-results} shows and analyzes the results. Here the primary and secondary research questions is answered through comparison with the published solvers, break the results down by instance characteristics, examine the learned \gls{haos} weights and the convergence of individual runs, and attribute the improvement to the stages that produced it.

\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:stu-experiment}
All 100 instances are solved by the same \gls{drispi} configuration. Nothing is tuned
per instance by design without any exceptions. Section~\ref{sec:stu-dataset} describes the instances and the reference solutions used as baseline, section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation} the software and hardware the campaign
ran on, section~\ref{sec:stu-hyperparameters} the parameter values, and
section~\ref{sec:stu-repetitions} the number of seeds and the statistical precision it buys for this experiment.

\subsection{Dataset and Reference Solutions}\label{sec:stu-dataset}
The XL set (section~\ref{sec:lit-problem}) extends the generation scheme of the X
instances of \citet{uchoaNewBenchmarkInstances2017} to up to 10,000 customers. Every instance
places its nodes on a $[0, 1000] \times [0, 1000]$ grid with Euclidean distances
and combines one level of each of four attributes. The depot is Random (R), Central
(C) at $(500, 500)$, or Eccentric (E) at $(0, 0)$ in the bottom left corner. Customers are Random (R), Clustered (C), or
Random-Clustered (RC) around a small number of seed points. Demand follows one of
seven distributions: unitary (U), four ranges of increasing width (1--10, 5--10,
1--100, 50--100), a quadrant-dependent range (Q), or many small and few large
demands (SL). The target average route length $r$ lies in one of seven bands,
from ultra-short ($r \in [3, 5]$) to ultra-long ($r \in [50, 200]$), and
capacity $Q$ is set so that a solution with the minimum number of routes attains
it. Figure~\ref{fig:unsolved-instances} draws one instance from each end of that range. XL-n3888-k1010 combines a random depot, customers clustered around two seed points, many small demands and a few large ones, and $r = 4.2$. XL-n9571-k55 combines a random depot, random customers, quadrant-dependent demand, and $r = 174.7$. The instance name references $n$ and that minimum route count, e.g.\
XL-n4535-k1134. This $k$ equals $\lceil \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i / Q \rceil$, making it the mathematical minimum number of routes, because fewer vehicles could not carry the total customer demand under capacity set to $Q$. This $k$ is
fixed by the benchmark and unrelated to the subcluster count $k$ of Section~\ref{sec:fra-haos}.
Table~\ref{tab:xl-summary} breaks down the above mentioned characteristics on a per-instance basis along with \gls{drispi}'s result and comparison to initial and current \gls{bks} values.

The initial \gls{bks} values of \citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} come from the solver
runs described in section~\ref{sec:lit-large-cvrp} and were released as baselines with the
CVRPLib \gls{bks} Challenge, a community competition opened on January~12, 2026, in
which any team can submit an improved solution for automatic verification. By
the current record,\footnote{\url{https://galgos.inf.puc-rio.br/cvrplib/index.php/en/bks_challenge/score/instances}, accessed September 7, 2026.} the challenge has improved 99 of the 100
initial values, leaving only XL-n1094-k157 to still hold its initial value. The
percentage gap is reported to baselines such as the initial and the current \gls{bks}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Gap} = 100 \times \frac{c - c_{\text{\gls{bks}}}}{c_{\text{\gls{bks}}}}.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The quantity $c$ is the reported solution cost. The current
\gls{bks} represents the official best known solution. However, the comparison is primarily against the solutions produced by the published solvers, because they originate from two-hour single-thread runs on specified hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:lit-large-cvrp}), using an experimental design that can be reproduced here to allow for any algorithmic comparability. The official \gls{bks} values, on the other hand, have been achieved without constraints regarding computational, with many of them coming from day-long runs that rely on raw compute, rendering any fair comparison infeasible.

\subsection{Implementation and Hardware Specifications}\label{sec:stu-implementation}
All experiments in this chapter run on a single, shared department server under
Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS with two Intel Xeon Platinum~8280L CPUs at 2.70\,GHz
(28 cores each, 56 cores and 112 threads total) and 5,928\,GiB of RAM, with no
GPU\@. \gls{drispi} runs inside Docker containers (Docker 28.1.1) pinned to a disjoint physical-core range with \texttt{--cpuset-cpus}. This pinned CPU count, not a library thread-pool default, sets the framework's parallelism budget.

The campaign presented in section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} is comprised of all 100 instances, each run on three seeds with a wallclock runtime budget and termination criterion of T=2h (300 runs, 7{,}200 seconds each). For that 48 of the server's 56 physical cores are pinned as six exclusive 8-core Docker containers, three per socket, parallelizing as outlined in section~\ref{sec:fra-pipeline} with a split into six \gls{dri} workers, one \gls{bg-ails} worker, and one \gls{sp} worker. SMT siblings are left idle. Runs are packed into waves ordered by instance size so that the three seeds of the largest instance, XL-n10001-k1570, never share a wave. The full campaign takes 50 waves and about 100 hours of wall-clock time.

\gls{drispi} uses Python 3.11, with dependencies locked via \texttt{uv}: Libraries PyVRP 0.13.3 (HGS-CVRP), scikit-learn 1.8.0 (K-Means and Agglomerative Clustering), scikit-learn-extra 0.3.0 (K-Medoids), scikit-fuzzy 0.5.0 (Fuzzy C-Means), gurobipy 13.0.1 (\gls{sc}/\gls{sp}), and vrplib 2.1.0 for instance parsing. AILS-II, FILO, and FILO2 are vendored as git submodules pinned to a fixed commit each and compiled at image build time, rather than resolved from a floating branch, so a rebuild reproduces the exact binaries behind the results in Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison}. The whole framework runs inside a Docker image built from this locked environment, so the software stack behind every reported run is fixed independently of the host it executes on.

\subsection{Replication Design}\label{sec:stu-repetitions}
The campaign runs \gls{drispi} on every one of the 100 XL instances three times, at
seeds 11, 22, and 33. The seed drives the weighted \gls{haos} draws and the random choices inside decomposition. It is also
passed to PyVRP, FILO, and FILO2, but AILS-II has no seed flag. This makes the routing phase, \gls{bg-ails} and the post-\gls{sc}/\gls{sp} improvement pass independent of the seed. This makes the runs non-reproducible. Additionally, the plugged-in solvers run under wall-clock limits, so how far their search gets depends on machine load, and the worker pool
schedules subcluster jobs in no fixed order. This affects again, the entire routing phase,
\gls{bg-ails}, and the post-\gls{sc}/\gls{sp} improvement pass alike, so two runs at the same seed
can differ in solution quality. Disclosing the seeds therefore does not suffice
for exact reproducibility. The three runs per instance are exchangeable replications.

Because each run is stopped after 7,200 seconds (120 minutes) of wall-clock time rather than at convergence, each run resembles a terminating simulation in the sense of \citet[p.~490]{lawSimulationModelingAnalysis2000}: a fixed initial condition, the first \gls{dri} iteration's decomposition and routing pass (Section~\ref{sec:fra-decomposition}), and a fixed stopping rule rather than a steady state. The replication method of \citet[p.~495]{lawSimulationModelingAnalysis2000} therefore applies.

The precision target refers to the fixed set of 100 instances, which are a design
factor \citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} rather than a sample from a larger
population, so the seed is the only randomness the design controls. Using
$\bar G$, the grand mean gap, and $\bar\sigma^2$, the average per-instance seed
variance, we get $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G) = \bar\sigma^2 / (I \cdot S)$ with
$I = 100$. The $I \cdot S$ observations are independent by construction. Every
run is a separate process with its own random-number stream, and no pool, cache,
or counter is shared across runs (Chapter~\ref{chapter:framework}). This interval does
not extend to the published baselines of Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison}, which
report one value per instance and no variance.

A pilot campaign of three diverse instances with four seeds each measured a
root-mean-square per-instance seed standard deviation of 0.118\,pp at the
7{,}200-second mark. The target half-width was fixed in advance at 0.03\,pp, small
enough to resolve the 0.02--0.06\,pp effects seen in earlier component tests.
Table~\ref{tab:seed-sizing} forecasts the standard error and the 95\%
half-width of $\bar G$ at the pilot value. Every $S$ from one to four meets the
target. This would in fact still hold true at a 99\% interval, but precision alone does not decide $S$.

Three other considerations set $S = 3$. At $S = 1$ there are no within-instance
degrees of freedom and no interval at all. At $S = 2$ a discrepancy between two
runs is visible, but cannot be attributed to either of them. At $S = 3$, however, one
anomalous run stands out against the other two. $S > 1$ is also required to present a best-of-$S$ column, which is the
convention on this instance set \citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026}.

A fourth seed would add 100 runs and 1,600 core-hours to shrink the half-width
from 0.013 to 0.012\,pp. That gain is too small next to a second source of error.
Two pilot campaigns that differed only in execution environment produced means
0.039\,pp apart, three times the $S = 3$ half-width. Such a batch effect shifts
every run of a campaign alike, so more seeds do not reduce it, and the budget of
a fourth seed would not buy a meaningfully more precise $\bar{G}$
\citep{barrDesigningReportingComputational1995,johnsonTheoreticiansGuideExperimental2002}.
Neyman allocation, which assigns seeds in proportion to each instance's standard
deviation (\citealp[pp.~97--98]{cochranSamplingTechniques1977}; \citealp{neymanTwoDifferentAspects1934}), would have further complicated the experimental design while only cutting the standard error of $\bar{G}$ by 8.8\% at the same total budget. Therefore, every instance receives
three seeds.

The pilot value was conservative. In the campaign, the mean per-instance seed
standard deviation is 0.037\,pp, about a third of the pilot value
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison}). The root-mean-square per-instance seed
standard deviation, which enters $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G)$, is 0.052\,pp, which
puts the realized 95\% half-width of $\bar G$ near 0.006\,pp. This
interval applies to the mean over seeds only. Best-of-$S$ is an extreme-value
statistic whose expectation depends on $S$, so it is not comparable across
studies with different replication counts. Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} uses
it descriptively and focuses its main comparison and the answer to this thesis's primary research questions on the mean-of-$S$ gap.

\subsection{Comparison with Monolithic Solvers}\label{sec:stu-comparison}

This section compares \gls{drispi} against the eight monolithic solvers \citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} benchmark on the 100 XL instances: AILS-II, FILO, FILO2, KGLS-XXL, HGS-CVRP, SISRs, LKH-3, and OR-Tools. The mean-of-$N$ and best-of-$N$ cost from the original paper reports ($N = 60$ for the published solvers, $N = 3$ for \gls{drispi}) represent the baseline, from which the gap to the current \gls{bks} is calculated. The per-instance gap is then averaged over all 100 instances for the main comparison. In a separate comparison, the mean-of-$N$ and best-of-$N$ gaps of each solver are compared on the fifty instances with fewer than 3,400 customers and the fifty with more, using the same split \citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} established for their own results table. \citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} report their own average gaps against the \gls{bks} at the time of publication, effectively AILS-II's own result for most instances, so those published averages are not comparable to the ones reported here. A detailed instance-level result breakdown of \gls{drispi}'s performance compared to the initial and current \gls{bks} columns can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:xl-results} (Table~\ref{tab:xl-summary}).

The mean-of-$N$ is used for the primary comparison in Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}, because \gls{drispi}'s mean-of-3 and each published solver's mean-of-60 estimate the same expected cost of a single run, and differ only in how many draws the average rests on. Best-of-$N$ on the other hand is an extreme-value statistic whose
expectation falls as $N$ grows. \gls{drispi}'s minimum over three draws stands
here against the minimum over sixty for every published solver, a sampling budget
twenty times larger, and the comparison rewards the larger budget before it
rewards the better algorithm.

The size of that mechanical advantage can be read off \gls{drispi}'s own runs.
Sampling one of the three seed costs at a time gives an expected best-of-1 gap
equal to the mean-of-3 gap, $0.296\%$. The average of the smaller cost in each
of the three retained seed pairs gives a best-of-2 gap at $0.273\%$. The actual
three-seed minimum then gives the best-of-3 gap at $0.264\%$ as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}. The first extra sample is worth $0.023$
percentage points (pp) and the second $0.009$ pp, and the sequence is still
falling at $N = 3$. AILS-II, FILO2, FILO, SISRs, KGLS-XXL, and HGS-CVRP gain
$0.07$, $0.09$, $0.10$, $0.16$, $0.21$, and $0.25$ pp respectively between
their own mean-of-60 and best-of-60 columns, which are even larger reductions drawn from many
more repetitions. \gls{drispi}'s mean per-instance seed standard deviation is
$0.037$ pp, so additional seeds narrow its best column by less than they narrow
a higher-variance baseline's. This is what produces the ranks in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}: \gls{drispi} ranks third by mean-of-$N$ and fourth
by best-of-$N$ without any change in the underlying solution quality, because
the two columns reward different things. Best-of-$N$ is reported here to match the
published solvers' own headline metric, not as the primary comparison.

For the mean-of-three runs to carry that weight, the three runs have to be exchangeable replications. The campaign seeds 11, 22, and 33 produce mean gaps of $0.291\%$, $0.298\%$, and $0.300\%$, an ordering a Friedman test \citep{demsVarStatisticalComparisonsClassifiers} over the 100 matched triples does not
separate from chance ($\chi^2 = 3.61$, $\mathrm{df} = 2$, $p = 0.164$). No
pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank test between any two seeds reaches significance
either, the smallest $p$ being $0.158$ for seeds 11 against 22. The performance order
also runs against the iteration throughput: seed 33 completed the most \gls{dri} iterations of the three (8,208 against 7,730 for seed 22 and 6,865 for seed 11) and still finished last. The per-instance seed standard deviation has
a mean of $0.037$ pp and a median of $0.026$ pp. Its root mean square, the
quantity that enters $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{G})$, is $0.052$ pp, and the realized
95\% half-width of the grand mean is $0.006$ pp
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-repetitions}). The three runs are therefore exchangeable at set level: both tests block on
instances and do not detect a seed acting as a systematic factor across the 100,
so the mean over them is a property of the configuration rather than of a
particular seed. Neither establishes that the three runs are interchangeable
on any individual instance, and three observations at two degrees of freedom
could not settle that either way. Instance-level exchangeability follows from
the design of Section~\ref{sec:stu-repetitions}, where every run is a separate
process with its own random-number stream and no pool, cache, or counter is
shared. The spread is also not uniform across the set. It runs from $0.0005$ pp
on XL-n4535-k1134 to $0.222$ pp on XL-n6034-k61, and it tracks route length
rather than instance size, a pattern Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics}
takes up with the gap itself.

The means presented in Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} do also not establish whether an
observed difference is real rather than an artifact of the 100 
instances. \gls{drispi}'s mean-of-3 column is tested against the corresponding
mean-of-60 column of five of the eight published solvers, AILS-II, FILO2, FILO,
SISRs, and HGS-CVRP, using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test
\citep{demsVarStatisticalComparisonsClassifiers}, treating the 100 instances as matched pairs. KGLS-XXL,
LKH-3, and OR-Tools are omitted, because their gaps in Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} are large enough that the paired comparison would not be informative.

Each baseline is tested twice, in opposite directions, because the two claims
the framework has to support are not the same claim. The first family asks
whether \gls{drispi} outperforms the other solvers, with $H_0$ stating that the distribution of paired
differences is not shifted below zero. The second asks whether \gls{drispi} is performing
worse, with $H_0$ stating that it is not shifted above zero. Rejecting the
first is evidence of an advantage, failing to reject the second is the weaker
statement that no disadvantage is detectable. The second of the two is what being competitive with a
state-of-the-art monolithic solver means on a fixed instance set. Each
family is corrected separately for its five tests with the Holm step-down
procedure \citep{demsVarStatisticalComparisonsClassifiers}, and Table~\ref{tab:paired-tests} reports both
adjusted $p$-values side by side. The paired differences are the per-instance mean
percentage gaps to the current \gls{bks}, and each test uses the normal
approximation to the signed-rank statistic without a continuity correction.

Table~\ref{tab:paired-tests} splits the five tested solvers into three
groups. \gls{drispi} is significantly better than SISRs and HGS-CVRP. The margin
is $0.286$\,pp against SISRs and $1.152$\,pp against HGS-CVRP, and \gls{drispi} is
ahead on 98 and 99 of the 100 instances. Against FILO the mean paired difference
is marginal at $-0.037$\,pp and the \mbox{Hodges--Lehmann} shift $-0.026$\,pp, with an uncorrected
95\% confidence interval of $[-0.060,\ -0.001]$, and the uncorrected signed-rank
test rejects at $p = 0.043$. The Holm correction over five baselines leaves it
at $0.064$. On 100 instances with a paired-difference standard deviation of
$0.203$ pp, the corrected test resolves a shift of about $0.066$ pp at 80\%
power, so a difference of the size observed here sits below what this instance
set can separate. \gls{drispi} is ahead of FILO on the point estimate and on 55
of the 100 instances, and the set is too small to call it. Against
FILO2 neither test rejects: the mean paired difference is $+0.003$ pp,
\gls{drispi} is ahead on 47 of 100, and there is no detectable difference in
either direction. Against AILS-II the second family rejects decisively.
\gls{drispi}'s mean-of-3 is $0.137$ pp behind AILS-II's mean-of-60 and ahead on
only 4 of 100 instances. AILS-II found 93 of the 100 initial \gls{bks} values
\citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} and remains the strongest solver on this set by a clear
margin. \gls{drispi}, being level with FILO2, and not separated from FILO
by the corrected test, places inside the leading group without
instance-specific tuning, which is the claim Chapter~\ref{chapter:introduction} sets out to support.

\subsection{Performance by Instance Characteristics}
\label{sec:stu-characteristics}

The XL instance set varies two candidate notions of scale at once: the customer
count $n$ and the target average route length $r$ (Section~\ref{sec:stu-dataset}).
The two are not interchangeable stand-ins for the same underlying quantity.
$r$ is not derived from $n$; it is an independent design factor of the
benchmark's own generation scheme, which fixes vehicle capacity $Q$ and fleet
size jointly so that a minimum-route solution attains the target $r$ for
whatever $n$ the instance happens to have, and the 100-instance design
deliberately crosses a wide range of $r$ against a wide range of $n$ rather
than letting one determine the other. Their sample correlation on this set is
$0.054$. For \gls{drispi} specifically the two also act on the pipeline
differently: $n$ sets how many customers decomposition has to distribute across
$k$ subclusters, while $r$ sets how many stops a single vehicle round trip
covers on average and, by construction, compresses the number of routes
relative to customers once it grows large.

A linear regression of the mean-of-3 gap on $n$ over all 100 instances gives a
slope of $3.12 \times 10^{-5}$ percentage points per customer ($t = 3.48$,
$p = 0.00076$, 95\% confidence interval
$[1.34 \times 10^{-5},\ 4.90 \times 10^{-5}]$). A regression that treats $n$
as the only scale variable can pick up the long-route instances through it, so
the same fit is repeated on the 86 instances with $r < 50$, including only the instances falling below the dataset's own
ultra-long route threshold (Section~\ref{sec:stu-dataset}). The slope falls to $1.65 \times 10^{-5}$
percentage points per customer ($t = 2.62$, $p = 0.011$, 95\% confidence
interval $[3.96 \times 10^{-6},\ 2.90 \times 10^{-5}]$). It stays positive and
significant, so the association with $n$ is not carried only by the 14
long-route instances, but it halves once they are removed.

Customer count and route length can be compared directly once each is measured
in sample standard deviations from its own mean. One standard deviation is
2,456 customers for $n$ and $40.4$ stops for $r$. On that common scale the
slope on $n$ is $0.077$ pp per standard deviation ($R^2 = 0.110$) and the slope
on $r$ is $0.133$ pp per standard deviation ($R^2 = 0.332$). This shows that route length
accounts for about three times as much of the variation in the gap, and its
slope per standard deviation is $1.7$ times the slope on $n$. A joint
regression on both standardized predictors leaves a partial slope of $0.070$ pp
per standard deviation for $n$ and $0.130$ pp for $r$, and the route-length
partial slope exceeds the customer-count partial slope by $0.060$ pp per
standard deviation ($t = 2.31$, $p = 0.023$, 95\% confidence interval
$[0.008,\ 0.111]$).

Capacity $Q$ and route length are set jointly by the generation scheme.
With $k \approx \sum_i q_i / Q$ the minimum fleet and $r \approx n/k$, the ratio
$Q/r$ approximates the average demand per customer
$\bar{q} = \sum_i q_i / n$. The regressions use $\bar{q}$ computed from the
instance files. On this set $\bar{q}$ ranges from $1$ on the unitary-demand
instances to $75.5$ on the 50--100 instances, and its logarithm correlates
with $r$ at only $-0.189$, which makes it a third axis rather than a
restatement of the first two.
On its own, standardized $\log \bar{q}$ has a slope of $0.062$ pp per standard
deviation ($R^2 = 0.072$).

Adding standardized $\log \bar{q}$ to the joint regression raises $R^2$ from
$0.423$ to $0.570$ and leaves three significant slopes: $0.147$ pp per standard
deviation for $r$ ($p = 4.8 \times 10^{-15}$), $0.090$ pp for $\log \bar{q}$
($p = 1.1 \times 10^{-7}$), and $0.070$ pp for $n$ ($p = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}$).
Route length remains the largest of the three, and customer count the smallest,
in every specification that was fitted.

Figure~\ref{fig:gap-scale} fits each predictor on its own. The slopes for
$r$ and $\log \bar{q}$ are smaller there than in the joint model because the
two are mildly negatively correlated ($-0.189$) while both raise the gap, so
each masks part of the other until both are in the model; the customer-count
slope moves the other way, from $0.077$ to $0.070$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/gap_scale_comparison.pdf}
\caption{Mean-of-3 Gap to the Current \gls{bks} Against Customer Count, Average Route Length, and Log Average Demand per Customer.}
\label{fig:gap-scale}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

The same asymmetry appears in a quantity the gap regressions do not measure.
Regressing the per-instance seed standard deviation on each standardized
predictor in turn gives $0.022$ pp per standard deviation for $r$
($p = 7.2 \times 10^{-11}$, $R^2 = 0.353$), $0.002$ pp for $n$ ($p = 0.60$,
$R^2 = 0.003$), and a slope indistinguishable from zero for $\log \bar{q}$
($p = 0.99$). In the joint model only $r$ survives, at $0.022$ pp per standard
deviation ($p = 4.7 \times 10^{-11}$). Instance size therefore moves the level of the
gap without moving its stability, whereas route length moves both.
Figure~\ref{fig:seed-spread} shows the two panels side by side. Mean seed
standard deviation is $0.093$ pp on the 14 instances with $r \geq 50$ against
$0.027$ pp on the other 86, a factor of $3.4$, and the same band carries a mean
gap of $0.574\%$ against $0.251\%$ (Mann-Whitney $p = 0.00066$). The
long-route instances behave as a separate regime rather than as the upper end
of a single trend, which is the reason the scale claim above is stated
conditional on $r$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/seed_spread.pdf}
\caption{Per-Instance Seed Standard Deviation Against Customer Count and Average Route Length.}
\label{fig:seed-spread}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

The concentration of the largest gaps on long routes is intuitive when considering what
decomposition does to a long route. Every clustering method in
Table~\ref{tab:decomp-methods} draws its partition from customer
coordinates or route centroids and the dissimilarity metric of
Equation~\ref{eq:decomp-dissimilarity}, without reference to which vehicle will
eventually need which customers. A long route spans a customer set spread
further apart on average than a short route serving a comparable number of
stops. And that same spatial spread makes it more likely that some clustering
cut line passes through the route rather than around it. Once a long route
straddles a cluster boundary this way, the routing stage
(Section~\ref{sec:fra-routing}) still solves each side of the cut to a good local
optimum in isolation, but the seam between them is exactly the kind of defect
\gls{bg-ails} exists to repair (Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails}). \gls{bg-ails} repairs every
seam in an instance under the same fixed chain length of $k$ cross-reconnects
and the same wall-clock budget of Equation~\ref{eq:bg-ails-budget} regardless of
how many of those seams cut through a long route, so an
instance whose long routes cross more boundaries has more repair work competing
for a budget that does not grow to match it. The variance result fits this phenomenon: a repair budget that is sometimes sufficient and sometimes not produces run-to-run spread, which is what the long-route band shows. 

These results point at a specific parameter rather than at the stage as a
whole. Equation~\ref{eq:bg-ails-budget} adapts the \gls{bg-ails} budget according to the
wall-clock time of the surrounding decompose-route stage. It tracks through the
per-subcluster budgets of Section~\ref{sec:fra-routing} subcluster
customer counts, so it scales with the cost of producing the decomposition
rather than with the amount of boundary or length of boundary-adjacent routes that the decomposition created. Sizing that from the boundary membership the framework already computes is the change Section~\ref{sec:con-future} proposes to improve on this central weakness of \gls{drispi}.

Figure~\ref{fig:characteristics-panels} analyzes the remaining instance characteristics against the gap: depot position, customer distribution, and demand distribution. Of the three, only demand distribution separates the groups. Kruskal-Wallis tests give $p = 0.153$ for depot position (three levels), $p = 0.180$ for customer distribution (three levels), and $p = 0.00085$ for demand distribution (seven levels). Under Holm correction across the three
tests the first two stay well above any conventional threshold (adjusted
$p = 0.305$ for both) and the third remains significant (adjusted
$p = 0.0025$). Depot position and customer distribution are also
non-significant after controlling for $n$ and $r$ (partial $F$-tests,
$p = 0.76$ and $p = 0.55$). Whether the depot sits at the center, in the
corner, or at a random point, and whether customers are scattered, clustered,
or both, does therefore not measurably change what \gls{drispi} achieves.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/characteristics_panels.pdf}
\caption{Mean-of-3 Gap to the Current \gls{bks} by Instance Characteristic.}
\label{fig:characteristics-panels}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

The demand-distribution effect is real but it is not a seventh independent
factor. The group means run from $0.127\%$ on the unitary instances to
$0.451\%$ on the quadrant-dependent ones, and the five classes in between
($0.250\%$ to $0.337\%$) do not separate from each other at all
(Kruskal-Wallis $p = 0.33$ on those 72 instances alone). What distinguishes
the two extremes is $\bar{q}$: unitary demand fixes $\bar{q} = 1$, which means capacity
is consumed one customer at a time, and a purely spatial partition is
automatically a balanced-load partition. The quadrant-dependent
distribution makes demand a function of position, where a partition drawn from
coordinates alone cuts along a demand gradient and hands subclusters very
unequal loads. Once standardized $\log \bar{q}$ enters the model alongside $n$
and $r$, the demand-distribution grouping no longer adds anything
($F = 1.52$, $p = 0.18$), and neither does depot position ($p = 0.22$) or
customer distribution ($p = 0.98$).

Additionally, each of the seven demand classes holds only 14 or 15 instances, leading to the
individual group means in Figure~\ref{fig:characteristics-panels} carrying the per-instance
imprecision discussed in Section~\ref{sec:con-limitations} and being reported
descriptively. The tests above are run on the full set.

In conclusion, \gls{drispi}'s gap to the \gls{bks} mixes two things: how hard the instance is for any solver, and
what additional costs decomposition adds on top of that. The per-instance mean-of-60 gaps of
AILS-II separate them. Fitting the same three standardized predictors to
AILS-II's own gap gives $0.091$ pp per standard deviation for $r$
($p = 6.5 \times 10^{-18}$), $0.046$ pp for $\log \bar{q}$, and $0.024$ pp for
$n$ ($p = 0.0061$). Longer routes are therefore hard for the monolithic solver
too, and nearly as strongly as they are for \gls{drispi}, while customer count moves
AILS-II's gap by about a third of what it moves \gls{drispi}'s.

Fitting the same model to the paired difference then isolates what additional cost decomposition imposes. All three slopes are positive and significant, and they are close to
one another: $0.055$ pp per standard deviation for $r$
($p = 3.9 \times 10^{-8}$), $0.046$ pp for $n$ ($p = 2.2 \times 10^{-6}$), and
$0.045$ pp for $\log \bar{q}$ ($p = 5.6 \times 10^{-6}$). Route length
dominates the raw gap because it is a factor expressing instance difficulty that happens to
hit \gls{drispi} harder than it hits AILS-II. Customer count dominates
neither, but what little it does to the raw gap is almost entirely a
decomposition cost rather than general difficulty. Over the full observed range
of the set, $3.6$ standard deviations of $n$ and $4.6$ of $r$, the
corresponding penalties relative to AILS-II are about $0.17$ pp and $0.25$ pp.
The weak scale dependence claimed for the gap therefore holds in absolute
terms and holds less comfortably in relative ones. The size split of Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} shows the same without a model: \gls{drispi} trails AILS-II by $0.097$ pp on the fifty instances below 3,400
customers and by $0.177$ pp on the fifty above. Reaching the leading group is
therefore better supported at the lower end of the XL range than at the upper.

\subsection[Learned HAOS Weights]{\texorpdfstring{Learned \gls{haos} Weights}{Learned HAOS Weights}}
\label{sec:stu-haos}

\gls{haos} chooses the number of subclusters $k$, the demand weight $\lambda_q$,
the decomposition paradigm, the clustering method, and the subsolver, one draw per \gls{dri}
iteration, and updates its weights from the rewards of
Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} (Section~\ref{sec:fra-haos}).  The clustering method is split in
two wheels, one per paradigm, so the five decisions are drawn from six decision wheels.  Whether
those weights carry interpretable information about an instance is the secondary
research question of this thesis, and answering it takes two steps.  A weight
vector that has left the uniform distribution has learned something only if what
it learned is the ranking of the arms.  A wheel can also leave uniform distribution by
reinforcing whichever arm its own early draws happened to favor, which produces
the same shape and means nothing.  The two are distinguishable, but not from the
weight vector alone. To separate them, it is then tested whether the arms that ended
up heaviest are the arms that actually paid off.  This section establishes the movement
first and then tests its direction.

\gls{drispi} provides per-iteration weight snapshots its runs log for all six
wheels.  Recomputing Equation~\ref{eq:haos-probability} from the logged raw
weights reproduces the logged probabilities to a maximum absolute deviation of
roughly $10^{-16}$ across 630,000 arm snapshots, so the weight trajectories
below are read rather than reconstructed.  The ten warm-up iterations are
excluded throughout.  Without them, runs complete between 22 and 118 iterations with a median
of 78, and 149 of the 300 exceed that median, so the later part of every
trajectory describes the longer half of the campaign.

Figure~\ref{fig:haos-entropy} shows normalized entropy staying high on five of
the six wheels. Normalized entropy goes from zero, at absolute singularity, to one, absolute uniformity. Figure~\ref{fig:haos-entropy} alone suggests the weights never leave a uniform distribution.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/haos_entropy.pdf}
\caption[Normalized Shannon Entropy of the Six HAOS Wheels Against DRI Iteration.]{Normalized Shannon Entropy of the Six \gls{haos} Wheels Against \gls{dri} Iteration.}
\label{fig:haos-entropy}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

But the weights do move, and the reward recurrence says how far they should. Credit
adds the reward earned on the iterations an arm is drawn. Decay removes a
fraction $1 - \gamma$ of its whole weight every iteration. With credit applied
before decay (Equations~\ref{eq:haos-update} and~\ref{eq:haos-decay}), an arm
drawn with a steady probability $p$ and paid $R$ per selection settles at

\begin{equation}
  w^{*} = \frac{\gamma\, p\, R}{1 - \gamma} = 19\, p\, R ,
  \label{eq:haos-fixedpoint}
\end{equation}

at the deployed decay factor $\gamma = 0.95$. Below it the
arm is pinned to the floor. At a uniform draw on a wheel with $A$ arms, $p = 1/A$, so the weight leaves the floor only when $R > w_0 (1 - \gamma)/(\gamma p) =
0.5263\,A$. The arm count is relevant because an arm is paid only on the iterations
it is drawn, and a wheel with more arms needs a larger reward per selection to
sustain its weight.

The wheels sit well above the decay floor. At iteration 78 the median ratio of the
largest weight on a wheel to $w_0$ is 4.89 for $k$, 4.65 for $\lambda_q$, 9.37
for the paradigm wheel, 2.85 for the vertex-method wheel, 3.36 for the
route-method wheel, and 6.87 for the subsolver. Arms only rarely sit exactly on
the decay floor, and that fraction does not rise with arm count. Nearly every
arm leaves the floor at some point in a run, and once credited it stays above
the floor for a mean of 31 to 40 snapshots against a re-selection gap of 1.9 to
7.8 iterations, so an arm is credited again long before it relaxes back.

And the probabilities follow the weights. The median top-arm probability at the
last iteration is 0.257 on the $k$ wheel against a uniform $1/7$ of 0.143, 0.323
on $\lambda_q$ against 0.167, 0.694 on the paradigm wheel against 0.500, 0.295
on the vertex-method wheel against 0.200, 0.481 on the route-method wheel
against 0.333, and 0.432 on the subsolver against 0.250.  Every wheel ends
between 1.4 and 1.9 times uniform on its leading arm.

Since it is now established that the arms move, the direction of movement needs to be investigated now. Because what the wheels have not done is rank the arms.  Within a run, the Spearman correlation between an arm's mean realized reward and that arm's final
probability is 0.081 on the $k$ wheel, 0.045 on $\lambda_q$, 0.043 on the
vertex-method wheel, 0.030 on the route-method wheel, and 0.042 on the
subsolver.  The arms that finish heavy are not the arms that produce high-quality solutions. Figure~\ref{fig:haos-scatter} plots the two against each other.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/haos_scatter.pdf}
  \caption{Mean Immediate Reward of Each Arm Against Its Final Wheel Probability. Each point represents one arm in one run with at least five draws.}
  \label{fig:haos-scatter}
\end{figure}

Part of that is a property of the reward set rather than of \gls{haos}. The mean within-run spread between the best and the worst arm is only 0.45 to 1.05 reward points against a median selection reward of 5.  On the instances and budget of this campaign the arms are close enough that no wheel has a strong ranking available to learn.

That assumption can be checked on a measure the reward scale does not bound.
Scoring every draw by the cost of the solution it produced relative to the
incumbent it started from, $100\,(C_t - c^{\mathrm{BG}}_t)/C_t$ and adjusted
for position in the run, the pooled best-to-worst arm spread clears the
$0.037$ pp seed-noise floor on five of the six wheels. That pooled ranking
does not survive a change of seed. On the $k$ wheel the best arm is 2, then 6,
then 10 across seeds 11, 22 and 33; on $\lambda_q$ it is 0.2, then 0.8, then
0.6; on the vertex-method wheel fuzzy c-means is the best arm on one seed and
the worst on another; on the subsolver AILS-II is the worst arm on seed 11 at
$-0.305$ pp and the best on seed 33 at $+0.028$ pp. Those pooled winners
average three sequences that disagree. The route-method wheel is consistent,
with agglomerative average best on every seed, but only marginally so, by $0.020$ to $0.039$ pp
against a floor of $0.037$. Only a single wheel carries a ranking that is both consistent
and large enough to matter. Route-based clustering beats vertex-based on every
seed, by $0.071$, $0.110$ and $0.065$ pp, and by more as the run progresses,
which is what the mechanism predicts, since route-based clustering partitions
the incumbent's own routes, which are worse earlier in the run.

The instances of one seed are not independent observations of an arm. Within a
seed the early draw sequence is nearly identical across instances, so the arm
drawn at a given iteration is the same on almost every instance of that seed,
and paired tests over instances overstate the precision of the pooled
comparison. Agreement across the seeds is the informative check, and it
is what the paragraph above uses.

That one wheel is where this section's account can be tested, and it fails in
the way the account predicts. The paradigm wheel is the wheel that
concentrates, its leading arm reaching a median probability of $0.694$; it has
the fewest arms, the lowest lift-off threshold, and a real advantage to find.
But it does not find it. At the last iteration the leading arm is the vertex-based paradigm on 154
runs and the route-based paradigm on 146. And the median final probability on the route-based paradigm, the better
arm, is $0.489$. What sets the leader is the seed and not the instance: seed 11
finishes on route in 99 of its 100 runs, seed 22 on vertex in 89, and seed 33
on vertex in 64, while the three seeds of one instance agree on the leader only in 8
instances out of 100. A wheel learning about an instance would agree with
itself across seeds. This one commits at roughly seven to three to whichever
arm its own early draws happened to favor.

That explains a mostly flat weight vector. It does not explain however why a weight vector that
has moved to 1.4 to 1.9 times uniform on an arm chosen at random. Figure~\ref{fig:haos-reward} gives the immediate reward $R$ banked by one
selection, summed over the decompose-route candidate and the \gls{bg-ails} candidate.
Of 19,803 post-warm-up selections, 16,591 bank exactly $R = 5$: the
decompose-route candidate scores the no-improvement value of 1 and \gls{bg-ails}
scores the improvement-over-previous-iteration value of 4.  A \gls{bg-ails} reward is present on
96.8\% of selections.  The median $R$ is 5 and the mean 4.94, and with 84\% of all
selections, almost all receive the identical reward.

\caption[Immediate (a) and Deferred (b) HAOS Rewards, Excluding the Ten Warm-Up Iterations.]{Immediate (a) and Deferred (b) \gls{haos} Rewards, Excluding the Ten Warm-Up Iterations.}
\label{fig:haos-reward}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

\gls{bg-ails} almost always improves on the
previous candidate, and almost never produces a
new incumbent best, so nearly every selection lands in the middle bucket.  The
factor that separates a good configuration from a mediocre one on this
benchmark is the size of the improvement, and the reward scale ignores it completely. An improvement of 1\% is rewarded the same as an improvement of 0.01\%.
With $R = 5$, the lift-off condition of Equation~\ref{eq:haos-fixedpoint} is
met comfortably: between 90\% and 98\% of selections clear $0.5263\,A$ on every
wheel except the ten-arm $k$ domain, where the threshold is $0.5263\,\times10 = 5.26$ and only 5.4\% clear it. Weights therefore rise on nearly every arm nearly every time it is drawn.

Because of that a missing ranking is turned into a confident-looking, but random weight vector.
Furthermore, the smallest reward for any feasible solution in Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} is 1.0 rather than zero, so
an arm accumulates weight just for having been drawn, independently of what followed.
But weight sets selection probability through Equation~\ref{eq:haos-probability},
selection probability sets the draw rate, and the draw rate sets the rate of
accumulation. An arm that is drawn a few extra times early therefore compounds that lead without any regard of its own performance. The result is reinforcement of sampling noise rather than of the outcomes, explaining every observation in this section at once: the entropy curves dip, the leading arm ends above uniform, the three seeds of one instance disagree at a total-variation distance of 0.2 to 0.3
(Figure~\ref{fig:haos-seeds}), and that disagreement does not fall as runs get
longer, because a reinforced random walk diverges rather than converges.

\label{fig:haos-seeds}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

But the weight level itself is not explained by the immediate reward alone. As shown, the interior fixed point of the recurrence in Equations~\ref{eq:haos-update}
and~\ref{eq:haos-decay}, for a stationary selection probability $p$ and a
per-selection reward $R$ under credit-then-decay, is
$w^{*} = \gamma p R / (1-\gamma) = 19\,pR$.  Evaluated at the run-average
probability and the mean immediate reward, that prediction sits below the
observed median maximum weight by a factor of 1.70 to 3.01 on every wheel. But two aspects are not considered here.

The first is that $p$ is not stationary.  Under the reinforcement above, the
leading arm's probability rises over the run, so a run-average $p$ actually understates the probability at which the largest weight was actually accumulated.  The
second is the deferred channel, which contributes 44.3\% of all credit mass on
every wheel across the entire run and does not appear in the immediate reward at all.  Correcting for both, total credit per selection is $4.94 / (1 - 0.443) = 8.87$, and evaluating $19\,pR$ at the median final top-arm probability gives 43.3 for $k$ against an observed 48.9, 54.4 for $\lambda_q$ against 46.5, 72.8 for the subsolver against 68.7, and 117.0 for the paradigm wheel against 93.7.  The divergence between the assumed and observed probability level originates from the weight level once it is given the probability the weight was accumulated at
and the full credit stream.  The two conditional method wheels need a further
correction for how often their paradigm was drawn and are not reconciled here.

That deferred channel as a mechanism actively works against
ranking.  The deferred reward is credited once to every distinct \gls{haos}
tag among the routes \gls{sc}/\gls{sp} selected, at the full value regardless of how many
routes that tag contributed (Section~\ref{sec:fra-sp}).  On an
iteration that carries a positive deferred reward, 1.8 to 5.3 arms of the same
wheel gain weight at once, against 0.55 to 1.34 on an ordinary iteration, and
about 62 weight units are injected against about 5.  Nearly half the credit in
the system arrives as a broadcast timed to the \gls{sc}/\gls{sp} schedule rather than as a
judgement about the arm that received it.

The secondary research question asked whether the learned weight distributions
give interpretable insight into instance structure and reduce manual tuning
across instance classes.  On this campaign they do not, and the diagnostics
above locate three causes rather than one.  First, the reward scale places 84\% of
selections in a single bucket and discards the improvement magnitude that would
separate the arms.  Second, the smallest reward is positive, so weight accumulates with the draw count and the roulette draw feeds that back, which converts sampling
noise into a stable-looking leader.  Third, the deferred channel supplies 44\% of the
credit as a broadcast to many arms at once.

None of the three is a property of the hierarchy.  The wheel mechanics behave
exactly as specified, the weights move freely, and the probabilities track them.
What the campaign shows is that the credit these wheels are fed does not carry a
ranking, and Section~\ref{sec:con-future} suggests what would have to
change.  The consequence for the primary research question is stated in
Section~\ref{sec:con-limitations}: a controller whose weights are uncorrelated with
arm performance is, in effect, close to the uniform draw its predecessor used,
so the result of Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} was obtained without
working adaptation rather than because of it.

\subsection{Convergence Behavior}
\label{sec:stu-convergence}

An improvement is any candidate that strictly lowers the incumbent cost,
including the first solution of a run. The run logs record 2,335 such updates
across the 300 runs. Figure~\ref{fig:improvement-quarters}(a) counts them by the
wall-clock quarter in which they were logged, measured from the first timestamp
of each run log. Every run's first solution is one of these updates, so naturally, 300 of them fall in the first quarter, and only 1,286 first-quarter updates lower an incumbent that already exists.

Simply counting updates overstates how much of the run is spent converging, because
the updates late in a run are more important, but smaller than the ones early in it.
Figure~\ref{fig:improvement-quarters}(b) therefore tracks the quantity the rest of this chapter reports. The mean-of-3 gap to the current \gls{bks} stands at $0.391\%$ at the 30-minute mark, $0.335\%$ at 60 minutes, $0.311\%$ at 90 minutes, against the $0.296\%$ of Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} once the 120 minute mark is reached. The
gains per quarter are $0.056$, $0.025$, and $0.014$ pp. While they are falling, they are not falling to zero: the last of the three is still about a quarter of
the first, and more than twice the realized 95\% half-width of the grand mean.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/improvement_quarters.pdf}
\caption{Convergence Over the 7,200-Second Budget. (a) Number of Incumbent Updates By Wall-Clock Mark, (b) Mean-of-3 Gap By Route Length to the Current \gls{bks} at Each Wall-Clock Mark}
\label{fig:improvement-quarters}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-\intextsep}

The run-level counts say the same thing more directly. Of the 300 runs, 122
improved their incumbent after the 90-minute mark and 221 after the 60-minute
mark. On 7 of the 100 instances all three seeds were still improving in the
final quarter, and on only 22 there was no seed improving anymore. At the point where the fixed budget of Section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation}
cuts the search off, \gls{drispi} is not at a converged solution on most instances.
A longer budget would have produced further gains, and the relevant question is how much. A geometric decay fitted to the observed quarter-on-quarter ratios of $0.45$ and $0.54$ implies a remaining tail of about $0.013$ pp beyond 120 minutes, which would put the set mean near $0.283\%$. That extrapolation rests on two ratios and is offered as an order of magnitude, not as a result. A long-run campaign to test it was not run.

This extrapolation says roughly how much improvement is left. Which instances
are still collecting it is the more useful question, and the answer is not the
one the completed iteration counts suggest. Completed \gls{dri} iterations fall sharply with instance size, from a mean of 117 on the ten smallest instances to 31 on
the ten largest (Spearman $\rho = -0.97$), and the natural reading is that the
largest instances are the ones furthest from convergence at the cutoff. The
improvement data does not support it. The mean-of-3 gain over the final
quarter correlates with route length at $\rho = +0.52$
($p = 3.9 \times 10^{-8}$) and with customer count at $\rho = +0.06$
($p = 0.57$). It averages $0.041$ pp on the 14 long-route instances against
$0.010$ pp on the other 86, while the split by size is almost flat: $0.015$ pp above
3,400 customers against $0.013$ pp below. Figure~\ref{fig:improvement-quarters}(b)
shows the two route lengths separately, and the long-route curve is still
descending visibly at the cutoff where the short-route curve has flattened.

The two findings are consistent once the binding constraint is stated
correctly. Fewer iterations on large instances leave \gls{haos} with fewer draws per
arm, but Section~\ref{sec:stu-haos} shows that the arms are statistically
indistinguishable on all six wheels, so a configuration drawn under a shorter
iteration budget is not inherently inferior. The iteration count
constrains what the wheels can learn. It does not by itself leave cost on the
table. Long routes leave cost on the table for the different reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics}: they generate more boundary repair
work than the fixed \gls{bg-ails} budget covers within one iteration, so the repair
continues across iterations and needs more wall-clock time. The instances with the largest gaps are therefore also the instances still
improving fastest when the run is stopped, which means part of the long-route
penalty reported above is a budget artifact rather than a defect of the
decomposition itself. How much of it is which was not tested here.

\subsection{Contribution by Stage}
\label{sec:stu-sources}

Sections~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} to~\ref{sec:stu-convergence} treat
\gls{drispi} as one procedure. It includes five stages, and the route pool tags every
route with the stage that produced it, so the improvement each stage contributes can be attributed rather than assumed. The
question matters for two reasons. The framework's contribution over its \gls{cvrp} 
predecessor \gls{drsci} \citep{altendeiteringDRSCICVRP2026} rests on \gls{bg-ails} and \gls{haos}, and the \gls{bg-ails} ablation of Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails} measured that stage in isolation
on a fixed checkpoint rather than inside a full 7,200-second run. And a stage that
carries only few of the total improvements under the campaign budget is also the
first candidate for reallocating core time.

The campaign log records 2,335 incumbent improvements. The 300 decompose-route solutions of the first iteration are each run's opening solution, and both the update counts and the cost reduction below exclude them. Cost reduction is measured from that opening solution to the final incumbent, so the 298 \gls{bg-ails} repairs logged on the same iteration are included. The remaining 2,035 updates are the denominator below. Stage time is the sum of the
per-stage timers over the whole run, including the first iteration. Those timers run concurrently on the core split of Section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation}, so the time shares below divide the recorded stage time and not the 7,200 second budget.

One attribution is not recoverable as two separate stages. The \gls{sc}/\gls{sp} job
(Section~\ref{sec:fra-sp}) solves the pool and then runs the post-\gls{sc}/\gls{sp} improvement pass
of Section~\ref{sec:fra-improve} on the result, and the incumbent update is
logged only after that pass. Every improvement logged under the name \texttt{standard\_ails}, the log label of the post-\gls{sc}/\gls{sp} improvement pass, therefore owes partial credit to its preceding \gls{sc}/\gls{sp} job. The two timers are logged separately, and Table~\ref{tab:improvement-sources}
reports them that way.
