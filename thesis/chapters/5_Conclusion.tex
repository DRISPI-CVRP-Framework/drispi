\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chapter:conclusion}

This thesis asked two questions. The primary one was whether an
algorithm-agnostic decomposition framework that learns its decomposition and
routing choices during a run can match state-of-the-art monolithic solvers on
very large \gls{cvrp} instances. The secondary one was whether the weights that
learning produces give interpretable insight into instance structure and reduce
manual tuning across instance classes. The campaign of
Chapter~\ref{chapter:study} answers the first affirmatively under a
fixed wall-clock budget and the second negatively, and the two answers turn out
to be connected: the framework reaches the leading group without the learning
having worked. Section~\ref{sec:con-findings} states both answers and what the
campaign shows about which parts of the framework do the work.
Section~\ref{sec:con-limitations} separates what limits the validity of those
measurements from what the study did not attempt, and
Section~\ref{sec:con-future} takes up what each of those gaps would need.

\section{Findings}
\label{sec:con-findings}

\gls{drispi} reaches a mean-of-3 gap of $0.296\%$ to the current \gls{bks} and $0.211\%$ to
the initial \gls{bks} across the 100 XL instances, which places it third of the nine
solvers in Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} by mean-of-$N$ and inside the
leading group. Against FILO2 the paired difference is $+0.003$\,pp and neither
one-sided test rejects. Against FILO it is $-0.037$\,pp in \gls{drispi}'s favour, on
55 of 100 instances, which the uncorrected signed-rank test separates at
$p = 0.043$ and the Holm correction does not
\citep{demsVarStatisticalComparisonsClassifiers}
(Table~\ref{tab:paired-tests}); on 100 instances with a paired-difference
standard deviation of $0.203$\,pp the corrected test resolves a shift of about
$0.066$\,pp, so a difference of the size observed sits below what this instance
set can separate. Against SISRs, KGLSXXL, HGS-CVRP, LKH-3, and OR-Tools the
margin is decisive. Against AILS-II, which found 93 of the 100 initial \gls{bks}
values \citep{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026}, \gls{drispi} is $0.137$\,pp behind and ahead on 4
instances. One best-of-3 cost, on XL-n3888-k1010, falls below the published
initial \gls{bks}, and on 11 instances a best-of-3 cost matches or beats AILS-II's
mean-of-60 (Table~\ref{tab:matches-beats}).

That comparison holds under a fixed wall-clock budget and not under equal
resources. Each \gls{drispi} run used eight pinned cores for 7,200 seconds, 16
core-hours against the two CPU-hours of each published run, at most 12.8 after
the PassMark scaling of Section~\ref{sec:con-validity}. Parallelism is
the premise of decomposition rather than an accident of the setup, and the
monolithic baselines are single-threaded by construction, so a comparison at
equal wall-clock is the one the research question asks for. It is still not a
statement about equal compute, and Section~\ref{sec:con-validity} gives
the full accounting. The defensible claim is that a decomposition framework
reaches the level of the leading monolithic solvers at this scale within a
practical time budget, using one configuration for all 100 instances and no
per-instance tuning.

The secondary question is answered negatively, and the diagnostics say more than
that. The HAOS wheels do leave the uniform distribution: their leading arm ends
between 1.4 and 1.9 times $1/A$ on every level. What they do not do is rank the
arms. Within a run, the correlation between an arm's realized reward and its
final probability is between $0.03$ and $0.08$
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-haos}), so the arms that finish heavy are not the arms
that paid. Three causes account for it, each measured: 84\% of selections bank
the identical reward, because the three-level scale of
Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} discards the improvement magnitude that separates
configurations; the smallest reward is positive, so weight accumulates with the
draw count and the roulette draw feeds that back; and the deferred channel
supplies 44\% of all credit mass as a broadcast to many arms at once. The
learned weights therefore carry no insight into instance structure on this
campaign, and no warm-start prior can be fitted to them.

Two measurements identify what does carry the framework. The controlled
comparison of Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails} isolates the improvement stage on
identical input: BG-AILS beats a stock AILS-II pass by $0.056$\,pp of cost
improvement (Hodges--Lehmann, 95\% confidence interval $[0.049,\ 0.063]$, ahead
on 85 of 100 instances) and a blind cross-reconnect control by $0.058$\,pp
($[0.052,\ 0.065]$, ahead on 89 of 100), while that control is itself
indistinguishable from the stock pass ($-0.004$\,pp, $[-0.011,\ 0.002]$).
Perturbing the concatenated solution buys nothing; guiding the perturbation to
the decomposition's own seams is what pays. Under the full campaign budget, with the opening decompose--route solution excluded,
Section~\ref{sec:stu-sources} credits BG-AILS with 88.2\% of the cost
reduction, from 48.8\% of the incumbent updates and 43.4\% of recorded stage time. The SC/SP-plus-standard-AILS package accounts for 8.8\% of that reduction and decompose--route for 3.0\%. The two figures answer
different questions, the first the margin over an alternative stage for the same
slot and the second the share of the reduction after the opening solution, and neither implies the other.
The ablation places the seam repair ahead of a stock pass on that slot. The campaign attributes most of the reduction after that opening solution to the same repair.

The remaining gap is not principally a matter of instance size. Three
continuous quantities account for 57\% of its variation across the set, and
route length is the largest of them at $0.147$\,pp per standard deviation against
$0.090$\,pp for the implied average demand per customer and $0.069$\,pp for the
customer count. Route length alone drives the run-to-run spread; customer count
and demand per customer do not move it at all. Depot position and customer
distribution are not significant before or after controlling for those three.
Setting \gls{drispi}'s per-instance gaps against AILS-II's separates instance
difficulty from what decomposing costs: long routes are hard for the monolithic
solver as well, and nearly as strongly, while the customer count moves AILS-II's
gap by about a third of what it moves \gls{drispi}'s
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics}). The scale dependence the primary
question asks about is therefore weak in absolute terms and less comfortably so
relative to the strongest baseline.

Four things follow that the thesis did not have before it. The first is the
performance result itself, obtained without instance-specific tuning on a
published, fixed, adversarially varied instance set. The second is the BG-AILS
mechanism, established causally rather than by construction, with the
perturbation confound excluded by its own control arm. The third is the account
of why an adaptive-operator-selection layer of this design fails on this problem
class, with the three causes separated and quantified; a controller that had
appeared to work without being examined this way would have been worth less.
The fourth is the measurement apparatus itself: the paired testing framework of
Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} with its resolution limit stated, and the
separation of instance hardness from decomposition cost in
Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics}, which is reusable on any decomposition
framework with a strong monolithic reference.

What the framework costs is CPU time and an unfinished search. The eight cores
do not deliver eight times the search of one, for the reasons
Section~\ref{sec:con-validity} sets out, and at the 7,200-second cutoff
122 of the 300 runs were still improving their incumbent
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-convergence}). \gls{drispi} is not reported at convergence.
It is reported at the point where a budget matched to the published solvers' own
per-run budget stopped it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:con-limitations}

The limitations fall into two kinds. Section~\ref{sec:con-validity}
covers what constrains how far the numbers of
Chapter~\ref{chapter:study} can be pushed.
Section~\ref{sec:con-scope} covers what the study did not attempt, and
each of those entries has a counterpart in
Section~\ref{sec:con-future}.

\subsection{Validity of the Comparison}
\label{sec:con-validity}

Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} compares runs that differ in hardware,
parallelism, and total budget, and none of its gaps correct for these
differences. \citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026} ran every published solver on a single thread
of a machine with two AMD EPYC~9654 processors (Zen~4, 2.40\,GHz), 60 seeds of
two hours per instance. \gls{drispi} ran three seeds of two hours per instance on
eight pinned cores of two Intel Xeon Platinum 8280L processors (Cascade Lake,
2.70\,GHz; Section~\ref{sec:stu-implementation}). Both machines were shared: the
published runs executed as up to 50 parallel jobs on one machine, \gls{drispi} as six
concurrent containers, three per socket, pinned to physical cores.

\citet{accorsiGuidelinesComputationalTesting2022} recommend bringing running times from different
processors to a common scale with the PassMark single-thread rating before
comparing them. The EPYC~9654 rates 2,898\footnote{\url{https://www.cpubenchmark.net/cpu.php?cpu=AMD+EPYC+9654&id=5088}, accessed September 22, 2026.} and the Platinum 8280L 2,316\footnote{\url{https://www.cpubenchmark.net/cpu.php?cpu=Intel+Xeon+Platinum+8280L+@+2.70GHz&id=6729}, accessed September 22, 2026.}, a
factor of 1.25. That factor is a lower bound: the 8280L rating rests on few
samples and is measured at the 4.0\,GHz single-core turbo, which the chip cannot
hold with 24 of its 28 cores busy. Scaled this way, \gls{drispi}'s 7,200 seconds per
run correspond to at most 5,800 seconds on the published solvers' processor, per
core. Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} reports unscaled gaps: \gls{drispi} was not
rerun with a longer limit to offset the slower hardware, and a single-thread
rating says nothing about how well eight cores are used in parallel.

The budget asymmetry points in opposite directions for the two columns of
Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary}. Per run, \gls{drispi} used 16 core-hours (at most
12.8 after PassMark scaling) against two CPU-hours for each published run, so
the mean-of-$N$ column compares runs in which \gls{drispi} had eight times the cores,
on older hardware. Those eight cores do not deliver eight times the search.
Subcluster routing runs in waves that leave \gls{dri} cores idle whenever the last
wave holds fewer than six subclusters, the main loop blocks while it waits for
BG-AILS (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-swimlane}), and three containers share the memory
bandwidth and last-level cache of each socket. Per instance, the relation
reverses. A published best-of-60 draws on 120 CPU-hours and 120 hours of
wall-clock time, \gls{drispi}'s best-of-3 on 48 core-hours (at most 38 after PassMark
scaling) and six hours. The minimum of 60 runs from a distribution lies
systematically below the minimum of three, so the best-of-$N$ column favours the
published solvers by construction. Neither column is an equal-resource ranking.

$S = 3$ was sized for the grand mean $\bar{G}$
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-repetitions}) and supports claims at that level only.
The forecast 95\% half-width of $\bar{G}$ is $0.013$\,pp. For a single instance,
the mean of three seeds at the pilot standard deviation of $0.118$\,pp has a
standard error of $0.068$\,pp and, with two degrees of freedom
($t_{0.975,2} = 4.30$), a 95\% half-width of about $0.29$\,pp. That is more than
twenty times wider, and wider still on the high-variance instances. The
per-instance results in Section~\ref{sec:stu-results} (the largest gaps in
Table~\ref{tab:gap-tail}, the instances where \gls{drispi}'s best-of-3 matches or
beats a published result in Table~\ref{tab:matches-beats}, and the seven
demand classes of 14 or 15 instances each in
Figure~\ref{fig:characteristics-panels}) are therefore descriptive. Across 100 instances,
some differences of that size will arise from the seed alone.

The instance set imposes its own resolution limit on the paired tests. With a
paired-difference standard deviation of $0.203$\,pp against FILO and 100
instances, the Holm-corrected test detects a shift of about $0.066$\,pp at 80\%
power, and roughly 310 instances would be needed for the observed $0.037$\,pp.
The verdicts of Table~\ref{tab:paired-tests} against FILO and FILO2 are
therefore statements about what this benchmark can separate as much as about the
solvers. The XL set is fixed at 100 instances, so more seeds do not help: the
variance that binds is across instances, not across runs.

The seed term is also not the largest source of error. Two pilot campaigns that
differed only in execution environment showed a batch effect of about $0.039$\,pp,
three times the $S = 3$ half-width, and more seeds would not have reduced
it. Shared-server load and Docker pinning are part of the measurement rather
than a nuisance the design controlled.

\subsection{Scope of the Study}
\label{sec:con-scope}

The 7,200-second limit matches the published per-run budget, not the run length
\gls{drispi} needs. At that cutoff 122 of the 300 runs were still improving their
incumbent and 221 had improved it within the final hour
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-convergence}). The residual tracks route length, with
a Spearman correlation of $+0.52$ ($p = 3.9 \times 10^{-8}$) against $+0.06$ for
the customer count ($p = 0.57$), so the instances carrying the largest gaps are
also the instances furthest from finishing. How much of the long-route penalty
reported in Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics} is a defect of the
decomposition and how much is a budget artifact was not separated, and no
campaign at a longer limit was run.

The iteration budget bounds how many draws each HAOS arm receives, and that
bound is tightest on the largest instances: completed iterations fall from about
117 on the ten smallest to about 31 on the ten largest
(Section~\ref{sec:stu-convergence}). A longer budget would not have
repaired the weights. Section~\ref{sec:stu-haos} finds the arms
statistically indistinguishable on this campaign and the arms finishing heavy
uncorrelated with the arms that paid, so additional draws would sharpen an
arbitrary leader rather than correct it. What the budget limits is the evidence
available to a controller that was not, on these runs, in a position to use it.

A control arm that freezes the draw at one configuration for the whole run under
the same budget was not run. The 300-run campaign behind
Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} used the server allocation available for
this thesis, and a controlled comparison needs a full campaign alongside it; a
subset carved out of the existing allocation would confound the two.
\citet{altendeiteringDRSCICVRP2026} leave the same gap for DRSCI, a \gls{drispi} predecessor
that draws paradigm, $k$, and solver uniformly at random each iteration without
learning. The weight diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:stu-haos} narrow what
that missing arm would have resolved. The wheels end between 1.4 and 1.9 times
uniform on their leading arm and the leading arm is uncorrelated with realized
reward, so the configurations this campaign drew are close to a uniform draw
with an arbitrary bias. The result of
Section~\ref{sec:stu-comparison} was obtained without working adaptation
rather than because of it, which makes that result robust to the controller and
removes online learning from what it demonstrates. What the missing arm would
still settle is whether drawing a fresh configuration every iteration costs
anything against one committed configuration, and that remains open.

Two further boundaries apply to the mechanism results. The BG-AILS comparison
of Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails} runs on one \gls{dri} iteration from a checkpoint under
one pinned decomposition configuration, so it establishes that boundary guidance
pays for that stage without establishing what the routes are worth once the pool
recombines them. The attribution of
Section~\ref{sec:stu-sources} covers the full campaign and partly closes
that gap, but it cannot separate the SC/SP solve from the standard-AILS pass
that adopts its result, because the incumbent update is logged only after the
pass. Finally, every claim in this thesis is conditioned on the fixed XL set of
\citet{queirogaXLInstancesCapacitated2026}. No instance outside it, and no \gls{cvrp} variant, was tested.

\section{Future Research}
\label{sec:con-future}

\subsection{Repairing the Controller}
\label{sec:con-controller}

Section~\ref{sec:stu-haos} locates three reasons the HAOS weights carry no
ranking, and each suggests a change that can be tested without altering the rest
of the framework.

The reward of Table~\ref{tab:haos-rewards} should be credited relative to a
baseline rather than absolutely \citep{fialhoAnalyzingBanditbasedAdaptive2010}. Crediting an arm with $R - \bar{R}$, the
running mean reward of recent draws on that wheel, leaves an average arm at zero
net credit and moves a good arm up and a weak one down. That removes the credit
an arm currently earns for having been drawn, which is what converts sampling
noise into a stable leader, and it requires no new signal.

The reward also needs resolution. Three levels place 84\% of selections in one
bucket, because BG-AILS nearly always improves on the previous candidate and
nearly never sets a new best. Scoring the normalized improvement
$(c_{\text{prev}} - c_{\text{new}})/c_{\text{prev}}$, or an arm's rank within a
sliding window of recent outcomes, keeps the magnitude that currently separates
configurations and is currently discarded.

The deferred channel should be normalized by the contribution it rewards.
Paying every distinct tag among the SC/SP-selected routes the full value credits
a configuration that supplied one route exactly as much as one that supplied
twenty, and it supplies 44\% of all credit mass. Dividing by the number of
routes a tag contributed, or holding the channel out until the first two changes
are in, would stop nearly half the credit arriving as a broadcast.

Credit assignment across the five levels remains open after all three. Every
arm of a drawn configuration currently receives the full reward, so each wheel's
signal carries the other four wheels' noise. With a baseline-subtracted,
resolved reward this becomes a variance problem rather than an absence of
signal, and the standard remedies apply: holding levels fixed in turn, or
estimating a per-level advantage.

Only with a reward that separates the arms does offline warm-starting become
possible. A prior fitted to instance characteristics would need weight vectors
that are stable across seeds, and this campaign does not produce them; the
three seeds of one instance end at a total-variation distance of $0.2$ to $0.3$ and do
not converge as runs lengthen. The prerequisite for that line of work is a
controller whose weights track arm performance, not a longer run of this one.

\subsection{Untested Comparisons}
\label{sec:con-comparisons}

Three campaigns would settle what Section~\ref{sec:con-scope} leaves
open, and none of them requires a change to the framework. A control arm that
commits to one configuration for the whole run, at the same budget and on the
same 100 instances, would measure what the adaptive draw costs or saves; with
the controller repaired as above, the same arm measures what it is worth. A
campaign at a longer wall-clock limit, four hours or more, would separate the
long-route penalty into the part decomposition causes and the part the budget
causes, which Section~\ref{sec:stu-convergence} can currently only bound.
An equal-compute comparison, running \gls{drispi} on one core against the published
single-thread baselines or the baselines on eight, would replace the
wall-clock-matched ranking of Table~\ref{tab:solver-summary} with a
resource-matched one. The last is the most informative and the most expensive.

\subsection{The Long-Route Regime}
\label{sec:con-longroute}

Section~\ref{sec:stu-characteristics} identifies route length as the largest
driver of both the gap and its run-to-run spread, and attributes it to a fixed
BG-AILS budget meeting a quantity of boundary repair work that grows with how
many seams cut through long routes. That suggests a budget for the stage that
scales with the realized boundary work of the current decomposition rather than
with the wall-clock time of the surrounding decompose-route stage
(Equation~\ref{eq:bg-ails-budget}), and a chain length that does the same. A
second direction is to make the decomposition itself aware of the incumbent's
route structure on these instances, which the route-based paradigm does only
partially, since it partitions routes without regard to their length.

\subsection{Transfer to Other Problem Classes}
\label{sec:con-transfer}

\gls{drispi} is agnostic about what solves a subcluster and about what partitions the
customers. Every clustering method in Table~\ref{tab:decomp-methods} and
every routing solver of Section~\ref{sec:fra-routing} sits behind a fixed interface,
and the set-partitioning recombination of Section~\ref{sec:fra-sp}
operates on routes rather than on anything \gls{cvrp}-specific. Extending the
framework to a variant therefore needs a subsolver that handles the variant and
a feasibility check on the recombination model, rather than a redesign of the
pipeline. The predecessor of this framework was built for a routing problem
with time windows \citep{kerscherDecomposerouteimproveFrameworkSolving2025} and adapted to the \gls{cvrp}, which is
evidence in the same direction. A monolithic solver offers no comparable route:
its scaling comes from engineering inside one search process, and that
engineering is specific to the constraints it was written for. Whether the
performance reported here survives that transfer was not tested, and the
boundary-repair mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:fra-bg-ails} is the part most likely to
need rework, since what counts as a seam depends on which constraints the
decomposition cut through.
